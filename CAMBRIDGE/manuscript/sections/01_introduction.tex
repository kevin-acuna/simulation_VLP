\IEEEPARstart{I}{ndoor} positioning is required wherever satellite navigation is unavailable. In warehouses, factories and hospitals, people, robots and drones must be located with an accuracy that satellite signals cannot provide. Radio-frequency technologies such as Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and ultra-wideband fill part of this gap~\cite{Zafari2019}, but multipath propagation limits their accuracy to the decimeter or meter level, and the most accurate of them require a dedicated infrastructure~\cite{Lymberopoulos2015,Leitch2023}. Visible light positioning (VLP) instead uses the light-emitting diode (LED) luminaires that already illuminate the building~\cite{Zhuang2018,Bastiaens2024}. Light is confined to the room, is immune to radio-frequency interference and reaches the receiver mainly through a line-of-sight path described by a simple radiometric model, so that centimeter-level accuracy is attainable with inexpensive components~\cite{Zhu2025}. The same luminaires can also transmit data, so that positioning and communication can share one infrastructure~\cite{Haas:16,Ma2022}. Among the possible measurements, the received signal strength (RSS) of a photodiode (PD) places the lowest demands on the hardware, as it requires neither synchronization nor an image sensor~\cite{Bastiaens2026}.

Most RSS-based VLP systems locate the receiver by trilateration. The RSS from each luminaire yields a distance, and the distances to three or four luminaires of known position determine the position of the receiver~\cite{Zhuang2018,Plets2019}. Several luminaires must therefore be in view simultaneously, each with its own identifier and its own calibration~\cite{Bastiaens2020}, which makes the positioning dependent on the lighting layout. This condition is often not met: a small room may have a single luminaire, and only one at a time is visible along a corridor or to a receiver with a narrow field of view. Even when several are in view, a single row of luminaires, as in a warehouse aisle, leaves the position across the row unresolved~\cite{Bastiaens2026}. Positioning with a single LED is therefore of practical interest~\cite{Wang2024}, since only one luminaire has to be visible, identified and calibrated, regardless of the layout of the existing installation. It is also a harder problem. A single RSS measurement is a scalar and cannot determine three coordinates, so the missing information must be supplied by some form of diversity.

A camera provides this diversity through the image of the luminaire~\cite{Hussain2022}, at the cost of an image sensor and its processing. With PDs, the usual solution is a receiver that combines several detectors with different orientations~\cite{Yang2014,Yu2018}. Some of these receivers resolve the three-dimensional (\mbox{3-D}) position~\cite{Yang2014}. A horizontal PD combined with three tilted ones yields it from the ratios of their RSS, as shown in simulation~\cite{Qin2020}, and prototypes based on neural networks report mean \mbox{3-D} errors of 6 to 8~cm at four receiver heights~\cite{Li2024} and, with an inertial measurement unit (IMU) to handle arbitrary orientations, of 1.77~cm~\cite{Ma2024}. Others estimate a two-dimensional (\mbox{2-D}) position on a known plane, with mean errors of 9.27~cm by fingerprint matching~\cite{Qin2021} and of 0.92~cm with a recurrent neural network~\cite{Chen2021}. All of them require several detectors and acquisition channels, whose gains and angular responses must be matched or calibrated individually.

A single PD can obtain the same diversity sequentially, by being reoriented. Huang \emph{et al.} mounted a tilted PD on a rotating platform and located it from the RSS measured at three rotation angles, with an average error below 2.5~cm in a $0.6\times0.6\times1.1$~m setup~\cite{Huang2015}. Two variants have since been studied. In the first, the PD is mounted on a rotating arm, and the position is obtained with the help of machine learning~\cite{Liu2022} or by least squares, with a Cram\'er--Rao bound used to select the arm length and the rotation angles~\cite{Shi2025}. In the second, the PD stays in place and only its orientation changes: the bound serves to choose the tilt, and the least-squares position is refined with a metaheuristic search~\cite{Wang2024} or by adapting the tilt to the estimated position~\cite{Shi2026}. All five systems estimate a \mbox{2-D} position and assume that the height of the receiver is known.

These works show that a single LED, and even a single PD, is sufficient, but three limitations restrict their use in practice. The first is the dimension of the estimate. A \mbox{2-D} position at a known height is adequate for a vehicle that moves on the floor, but not for a drone or a hand-held device, whose height is itself unknown~\cite{Plets2019,Bastiaens2026}, and an error in the assumed height propagates to the horizontal coordinates. The second limitation is the dependence on training data. Fingerprinting and neural networks~\cite{Qin2021,Chen2021,Li2024,Ma2024,Liu2022} learn the RSS of one particular installation. They outperform model-based estimators when a large dataset is available, but not when only a few measurements are~\cite{Glass2021}. The dataset has to be recorded at known positions throughout the working volume, for instance 45\,000 samples at 2\,250 positions in~\cite{Ma2024}, and recorded again whenever the luminaire, the receiver or the surroundings change. This effort, added to the cost of the training itself~\cite{Rekkas2023}, makes fingerprint-based positioning usually infeasible in practice~\cite{Bastiaens2026}.

The third limitation concerns the models and their validation. In the single-PD systems, the receiver is described by the ideal cosine response, whereas commercial PDs depart from it~\cite{Bastiaens2020}, just as real luminaires depart from the Lambertian pattern~\cite{Ding2016}. This matters particularly for a reoriented PD, which infers the direction of the LED from the variation of the RSS between orientations. The orientations are uniformly spaced and assumed to be exactly known, and the effect of their errors has received little attention. The experimental validations remain at bench scale, with working areas of about half a meter, distances to the LED of about one meter or less and a few tens of test positions~\cite{Huang2015,Shi2025,Wang2024}.

This paper addresses these limitations with a single fixed LED and a single tilted PD that is reoriented to $K$ known orientations. All three coordinates of the receiver are estimated from the $K$ RSS measurements: their normalized profile over the orientations gives the direction to the LED, and their common amplitude gives the distance through a calibrated model of the LED. The estimation is model-based and requires neither fingerprints nor training: its few parameters are calibrated from a small number of measurements under a single luminaire rather than from a survey of the working volume, which facilitates deployment. Neither a second detector nor an additional measurement is needed, which suits platforms where size, weight and power are scarce. A drone, for which a single PD is an inherently lightweight sensor~\cite{Bastiaens2021}, could obtain the orientations by rotating about its vertical axis while it hovers, with its attitude given by its inertial sensors. The main contributions are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
 \item A \mbox{3-D} formulation, with the height of the receiver unknown, for an arbitrary angular response of the PD and arbitrary known orientations. We derive its Fisher information, identifiability condition and position error bound, extend the bound to orientation errors, and draw from it two rules for the tilt of the PD.
 \item Model-based estimators for a general response: a maximum-likelihood estimator and two ratio-based least-squares estimators of the direction to the LED. The latter retain the closed form of~\cite{Acuna2026} for cosine-power responses and are solved iteratively for responses that cannot be linearized, such as the Square (SQ) model of commercial PDs~\cite{Bastiaens2020}. The distance follows in closed form from the same measurements, and the direction, which requires no calibration of the LED, also allows the receiver to be pointed toward the LED.
 \item A calibration that requires few measurements and no training data. A profile-consistency test selects the response model of the PD without using the gain or the pattern of the LED, which are then calibrated with the PD facing up.
 \item An experimental validation with a ceiling-mounted LED 3~m above the floor, on an automated testbed. Centimeter-level \mbox{3-D} accuracy is obtained on a grid of positions at several heights and in a blind test at random positions estimated live. The evaluation is repeated with three other LEDs, and the same data reveal the position and the tilt of each LED.
 \item An analysis of what limits the accuracy and of what the system requires. The response model of the PD is decisive, and the remaining error originates from small model errors, notably the departure of the LED from the Lambertian pattern, rather than from noise. The requirements on the orientations, which need not be uniformly spaced, and on the observation time are quantified.
\end{itemize}
