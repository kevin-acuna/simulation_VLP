This paper showed, in theory and in experiments, that a single fixed LED and a single photodiode reoriented to a few known orientations determine the three-dimensional position of the receiver, including its height. The measurement model separates a direction, given by the normalized RSS profile over the orientations, from a distance, given by their common amplitude through the calibrated gain and pattern of the LED. Both are obtained with model-based estimators, derived for a general receiver response, that require neither fingerprints nor training. The Fisher information makes the conditions explicit: the receiver must be tilted with respect to its rotation axis, the slope of its angular response sets the attainable accuracy, and orientation errors act as noise that a longer observation cannot remove. The resulting bound gives two rules for the tilt, which must be large enough for the orientation uncertainty and small enough for every orientation to keep the LED in view.

With a ceiling-mounted LED 3~m above the floor, the calibrated system reached a mean \mbox{3-D} error of 3.66~cm over 125 positions at five heights, and of 4.10~cm over 131 blind random positions estimated live with five orientations. The same accuracy is obtained with 10~ms of signal per orientation instead of 100~ms. The receiver response is decisive (77~cm with a Lambertian receiver), and nine flat-PD points over the working area calibrate the LED as well as a dense map. The remaining error is due to model bias, not to noise, and it is largest below the LED, where the radiation pattern departs from the calibrated Lambertian model. The orientations need not be uniformly spaced, but they must be known to about $0.2^\circ$. The positioning data also revealed the position and tilt of every LED in the room. Better models of the LED pattern and of the receiver orientation, a receiver reoriented by the platform that carries it, such as a mobile robot or a drone, the use of the positioning tone as a pilot of a communication link, and the combination of several LEDs are the next steps.
