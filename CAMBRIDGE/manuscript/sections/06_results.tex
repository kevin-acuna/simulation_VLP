This section reports the positioning accuracy, measured by the \mbox{3-D} error $\norm{\hat{\vect r}-\vect r}$, its horizontal component, the signed vertical error (positive when the estimate is above the true position) and the angle between $\hat{\vect u}$ and the true direction. The statistics are the mean, median, root-mean-square error (RMSE), 90th percentile (P90) and maximum. Section~\ref{sec:analysis} then analyzes what limits the accuracy and what the system requires.

\subsection{Receiver Models and Estimators}
\label{subsec:res-models}
For the PDA100A2, whose angular response was measured in~\cite{Bastiaens2020}, the published parameters are $\psi_{3\mathrm{dB}}=0.61$~rad for the SQ response~\eqref{eq:sq-response}, which therefore vanishes at $64.6^\circ$, and $m_R=1.9$ for the fitted cosine power. Table~\ref{tab:models} compares these two models and the Lambertian receiver $\cos\psi$ on D2. The profile-consistency test~\eqref{eq:shape} at the true positions does not involve $A$, $m$ or the LED pattern. It gives a residual of 0.80\,\% for SQ, 1.42\,\% for $\cos^{1.9}$ and 6.63\,\% for $\cos$, with the stage zero fitted for each model. The positioning results follow the same order. The Lambertian receiver $\cos\psi$ underestimates the decay of the response with the incidence angle: it places the LED at a much larger zenith angle (direction errors of $11^\circ$--$12^\circ$), and the range stage converts this into errors of 0.7--0.8~m. GLS and WLS then give no valid estimate at 10 and 9 of the 125 positions. The cosine order fitted to the same device reduces the error to 8.2~cm, still more than twice that of SQ. A model that fits amplitudes reasonably well can still misrepresent their slopes (Section~\ref{subsec:fim}), and the profile-consistency test exposes it.
With the SQ response, GLS and NLS agree within 0.01~cm, as expected at high SNR, whereas WLS ignores the correlation between ratios that share a reference and is slightly worse. Unless stated otherwise, positions are estimated in the following with the SQ response and the NLS estimator~\eqref{eq:nls}, followed by~\eqref{eq:range}.

\subsection{Accuracy on the Regular Grid}
\label{subsec:res-grid}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} retraces how the final accuracy was reached on D2, adding one model element at a time. Each row recalibrates $A$ and $m$ on D1 with the corresponding response and LED position. Starting from the conventional Lambertian receiver, the nominal stage zero and the LED placed at the maximum of the D1 map, the mean error is 77~cm. The published cosine order brings it to 8.9~cm and the SQ response to 4.98~cm. The calibrated stage zero lowers it to 3.91~cm, mainly through the horizontal error. Finally, locating the LED by the joint fit of the D1 map gives 3.66~cm. With this model, the median error is 3.40~cm, the RMSE 3.96~cm, the P90 6.06~cm and the maximum 7.24~cm. The mean horizontal error is 2.45~cm, the mean vertical error $-1.60$~cm and the mean direction error $0.75^\circ$.

The error depends on the height of the receiver (Fig.~\ref{fig:height}). It grows from 2.64~cm at the highest level (1.315~m, closest to the LED) to 4.42~cm at the lowest (0.955~m), and the vertical error drifts from $+0.03$ to $-2.66$~cm. The horizontal error, 1.9--2.8~cm, repeats at the same horizontal positions across heights. Both observations point to systematic model errors rather than noise, and the position of the receiver under the LED shows where they come from.

Within $15^\circ$ of the LED axis the mean error is 5.2~cm, against 2.8~cm beyond $20^\circ$, although the signal is strongest there and the noise-limited bound of Section~\ref{subsec:res-bound} the lowest (0.7 against 1.8~mm). Two effects add up. First, the LED does not follow the calibrated cosine power at small irradiance angles: between $10^\circ$ and $15^\circ$, the amplitudes fitted at the true positions of D2 lie 3.6\,\% below the model, as in the flat scan D1, which alone lengthens the estimated distance by about 3.8~cm. Second, close to the axis every orientation sees the LED at nearly the same incidence angle, so that the normalized RSS profile varies by only 15\,\% over the orientations, against 49\,\% beyond $20^\circ$, and the same small model errors turn into a direction error of $1.2^\circ$ instead of $0.5^\circ$. A lower receiver has more of its positions inside this cone, which explains the dependence on the height. Luminaires often depart from the Lambertian pattern~\cite{Ding2016,Bastiaens2026}, so this situation is likely to be common.

\subsection{Number of Orientations}
\label{subsec:res-K}
Subsampling the ten angles of D2 into near-uniform subsets of $K$ angles, with all their rotations, shows how few orientations suffice (Fig.~\ref{fig:cdf}). The mean error is 3.66~cm with $K=10$, 3.73~cm with $K=5$ (P90 5.97~cm), 3.97~cm with $K=4$ and 4.94~cm with $K=3$ (P90 8.07~cm). Beyond $K=5$ the additional orientations do not reduce an error that is dominated by model bias. With $K=3$ the problem is exactly determined and no redundancy is left against these biases, which shows mainly in the tail of the distribution. The orientations need not be uniformly spaced either. Over all 252 subsets of five of the ten angles, most of them irregular, the mean error is 4.56~cm. It grows with the largest azimuthal gap of the set: 3.73~cm for the two uniform subsets (gap of $72^\circ$), 4.06 and 4.52~cm for gaps of $108^\circ$ and $144^\circ$, and 5.40~cm when half a turn is missing. The best irregular subset reaches 3.55~cm. A receiver that turns irregularly, or only partially, thus remains usable, provided that its orientations are known.

\subsection{Calibration Budget}
\label{subsec:res-budget}
Because the direction and amplitude of every position do not depend on $A$ and $m$, the error on D2 can be recomputed exactly for any calibration. This is done here for calibrations obtained from $N$ points of D1 with the LED position known. A regular $n\times n$ grid over the working area reaches the accuracy of the full map with very few points: 3.45, 3.54 and 3.56~cm for $2\times2$, $3\times3$ and $4\times4$, against 3.66~cm with all 19\,881 points and with the staged calibration of Table~\ref{tab:parameters}. Random points over the area need about ten to approach it (median 3.85~cm for $N=10$). Concentrating the points around the LED does not work: a $3\times3$ grid with 0.1 or 0.2~m spacing centered on the LED gives 21.8 and 17.3~cm, and it recovers only when the spacing reaches 0.4~m (4.16~cm). The reason is that, near the axis, the measured product decays faster than the cosine power that it follows beyond $20^\circ$: the points there push $m$ upward, and the calibration must extend to the angles at which the order is stable. No pair $(A,m)$ does better than 3.14~cm on D2, even when chosen with the ground truth. This is the floor of the single-order pattern model, reached within 0.5~cm by nine well-placed points. The position of the LED need not be known beforehand either. Fitted on the same nine points together with $A$ and $m$, it lies 3~mm from that of Table~\ref{tab:parameters}, and the error on D2 is 3.47~cm. Nine flat-PD measurements thus provide the complete calibration of an LED.

\subsection{Blind Random Test}
\label{subsec:res-blind}
On the 131 random positions of D3, estimated live with $K=5$ and the calibration frozen beforehand, the mean \mbox{3-D} error is 4.10~cm (95\,\% bootstrap confidence interval 3.77--4.43~cm, median 3.91~cm, RMSE 4.53~cm, P90 6.73~cm, maximum 12.5~cm), with a mean horizontal error of 2.64~cm, a mean vertical error of $-1.96$~cm and a mean direction error of $0.90^\circ$ (Figs.~\ref{fig:cdf} and~\ref{fig:random}). Recomputing these estimates offline from the stored RSS reproduces the live values within 0.002~cm. With the nine-point calibration of Section~\ref{subsec:res-budget}, the same RSS give 3.97~cm. The result is close to the $K=5$ subsets of D2 (3.73~cm). The error is slightly larger, and its tail longer, mainly at the outermost positions (5.2~cm on average where $X$ or $Y$ is below 0.25~m or above 1.15~m, against 3.4~cm elsewhere). Moreover, unlike on D2, the stage zero was not fitted on the test data. The accuracy measured on the regular grid thus transfers to unseen positions.


\subsection{The Other LEDs as Single-LED Systems}
\label{subsec:res-leds}
The procedure is now repeated from scratch with the three other LEDs of the room, at 300, 700 and 900~kHz, whose tones are recorded in every measurement of D1 and D2. Each is treated as a new single-LED system: its position, $A$ and $m$ are fitted on the nine points of the $3\times3$ grid of D1, as in Section~\ref{subsec:res-budget}, whereas the receiver keeps the SQ response and the stage zero of Table~\ref{tab:parameters}, which do not depend on the LED. The 125 positions of D2 are then estimated with that LED alone. No parameter is adjusted on the RSS that these LEDs give on D2, which is therefore a blind test for them.

The mean \mbox{3-D} error is 7.1~cm with the 300~kHz LED, 13.0~cm with the 900~kHz LED and 24.5~cm with the 700~kHz LED, against 3.5~cm with the positioning LED calibrated in the same way (Section~\ref{subsec:res-budget}). The receiver model is not the cause: the directions that an LED gives at the known positions of D2, which do not depend on its gain or pattern, intersect at one point, its geometric position, and they pass within $0.6^\circ$ of it on average for the four LEDs alike. The cause is the LED model. The geometric position does not coincide with the calibrated one, which is the center of the pattern on the scan plane (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}). For the 700~kHz LED the two lie 18.9~cm apart, which corresponds to a tilt of about $5.8^\circ$ of its optical axis over the 1.865~m between the LED and the scan plane, whereas the tilts of the 300 and 900~kHz LEDs are $0.6^\circ$ and $1.7^\circ$, and that of the positioning LED $0.8^\circ$. The model assumes a vertical axis, so a tilted LED biases the calibrated pattern, and no number of calibration points can compensate for it. With the LED at its geometric position, triangulated at each height from the other four, the errors decrease to 6.2~cm (300~kHz), 6.6~cm (900~kHz) and 17.0~cm (700~kHz), but only a model with the LED tilt, or a realigned LED, would remove the bias~\cite{Bastiaens2026}. The 300 and 900~kHz LEDs are also seen at up to $42^\circ$ from their axes, against $38^\circ$ for the positioning LED, and their errors exceed 13~cm at the positions beyond $35^\circ$, where one orientation approaches the cutoff of the response. The triangulated LED heights, 2.95--2.97~m, are 3--5~cm below the \del{tape}\add{measured} value, a discrepancy discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:res-bound}. The method thus also measures where each LED is and where it points, from the same data used for positioning.
