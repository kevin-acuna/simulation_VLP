This section characterizes the testbed and reports the positioning accuracy on the three datasets; Section~\ref{sec:analysis} then analyzes what limits it and what the system requires.

\subsection{Measurement Noise}
\label{subsec:res-noise}
Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum} shows the spectrum of a 100-ms record of D2: the four tones stand out over a flat floor, and the flicker of the room lights is confined to low frequencies. The noise of the RSS, measured as the standard deviation over consecutive 100-ms records at fixed positions, is $\sigma=3.2~\mu$V at 500~kHz. Over D2, the 500~kHz RSS ranges from 1.07 to 20.7~mV, which corresponds to an SNR of 50 to 76~dB.

\subsection{LED Radiation Patterns}
\label{subsec:res-env}
Fig.~\ref{fig:maps} shows the flat-PD maps of D1 for the four LEDs. Each LED has a single smooth lobe, without visible reflections, and the joint fit of~\eqref{eq:led-calibration} locates the center of its pattern ($+$). For the 500~kHz LED this center is at $(281.1,904.8)$~mm, the value of Table~\ref{tab:parameters}.

The pattern does not follow a single cosine power, however. Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}(a) shows the apparent order of each point of D1, \add{that is, the value of $m$ that satisfies~\eqref{eq:led-calibration} at that point} \del{computed }with $A$ fixed by the point closest to the axis. It decreases from about 11 near the axis to a stable value of 6.0 beyond $\phi\approx20^\circ$. The measured product $R(\phi)\cos^m\phi$ is thus more peaked near the axis than the model; the data constrain only this product, so the deviation may originate in the LED optics, in the response of the PD near normal incidence, or in a slight tilt of the LED. The staged calibration gives $m=6.09$, close to the value beyond $20^\circ$, where most positions of the working area are seen; a joint fit over the whole map gives $A=0.0611$~V\,m$^2$ and $m=5.79$.

\subsection{Receiver Response Model}
\label{subsec:res-model}
For the PDA100A2, the published parameters are $\psi_{3\mathrm{dB}}=0.61$~rad for the SQ response~\eqref{eq:sq-response}, which therefore vanishes at $64.6^\circ$, and $m_R=1.9$ for the fitted cosine power~\cite{Bastiaens2020}. \add{Fig.~\ref{fig:response} shows these two models and the Lambertian receiver $\cos\psi$, together with the RSS of D2 divided by the amplitude fitted at each position. Over the incidence angles visited, from $2^\circ$ to $52^\circ$, the measurements follow the SQ curve.} Table~\ref{tab:models} compares \del{these two models and the Lambertian receiver $\cos\psi$}\add{the three models} on D2. The profile-consistency test~\eqref{eq:shape} at the true positions does not involve $A$, $m$ or the LED pattern; it gives a residual of 0.80\,\% for SQ, 1.42\,\% for $\cos^{1.9}$ and 6.63\,\% for $\cos$, with the stage zero fitted for each model\del{ (without this fit, 0.88, 1.47 and 6.63\,\%)}. The positioning results follow the same order. The Lambertian receiver $\cos\psi$ underestimates the decay of the response with the incidence angle\del{; to}\add{. To} explain the measured contrast between orientations\add{,} it places the LED at a much larger zenith angle (direction errors of $11^\circ$--$12^\circ$), and the range stage converts this into errors of 0.7--0.8~m\del{;}\add{.} GLS and WLS \add{then} give no valid estimate at 10 and 9 of the 125 positions, the only failures in Table~\ref{tab:models}. The cosine order fitted to the same device~\cite{Bastiaens2020} reduces the error to 8.2~cm, still more than twice that of SQ, with a vertical bias of $-5.6$~cm. A model that fits amplitudes reasonably well can still misrepresent their slopes (Section~\ref{subsec:fim}), and the profile-consistency test exposes it.

With the SQ response, GLS and NLS agree within 0.01~cm, as expected at high SNR; WLS ignores the correlation between ratios that share a reference and is slightly worse. NLS is used in the following.

\subsection{Accuracy on the Regular Grid}
\label{subsec:res-grid}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} retraces how the final accuracy was reached on D2, adding one model element at a time; each row recalibrates $A$ and $m$ on D1 with the corresponding response and LED position. Starting from the conventional Lambertian receiver, an uncalibrated stage zero and the LED placed at the maximum of the D1 map, the mean error is 77~cm. The calibrated cosine order brings it to 8.9~cm and the SQ response to 4.98~cm. Calibrating the zero of the stage with the profile-consistency test, which corrects it by about $1.5^\circ$, lowers it to 3.91~cm, mainly through the horizontal error. Finally, locating the LED by the joint fit of the D1 map gives 3.66~cm (95\,\% bootstrap confidence interval of the mean, 3.40--3.93~cm). With this model, the median error is 3.40~cm, the RMSE 3.96~cm, the P90 6.06~cm and the maximum 7.24~cm; the mean horizontal error is 2.45~cm, the mean vertical error $-1.60$~cm and the mean direction error $0.75^\circ$.

The error depends on the height of the receiver (Fig.~\ref{fig:height}). It grows from 2.64~cm at the highest level (1.315~m, closest to the LED) to 4.42~cm at the lowest (0.955~m), and the vertical error drifts from $+0.03$ to $-2.66$~cm. Both observations point to systematic model errors rather than noise\del{: the vertical drift is consistent with a pattern model whose effective order changes with distance, and the horizontal pattern with a small bias in the estimated directions}\add{, and the position of the receiver under the LED shows where they come from}.

\add{Within $15^\circ$ of the LED axis the mean error is 5.2~cm, against 2.8~cm beyond $20^\circ$, although the signal is strongest near the axis and the noise-limited bound is the lowest there (0.7 against 1.8~mm). Two effects add up. First, the LED does not follow the calibrated cosine power at small emission angles (Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}(a)): between $10^\circ$ and $15^\circ$, the amplitudes fitted at the true positions of D2 lie 3.6\,\% below the model, exactly as in the flat scan D1, which alone would lengthen the estimated distance by about 3.8~cm. Second, close to the axis every orientation sees the LED at nearly the same incidence angle, so that the normalized RSS profile varies by only 15\,\% over the orientations, against 49\,\% beyond $20^\circ$. \subsection{Number of Orientations}
\label{subsec:res-K}
Subsampling the ten angles of D2 shows how few orientations suffice (Fig.~\ref{fig:cdf}). The mean error is 3.66~cm with $K=10$, 3.73~cm with $K=5$ (P90 5.97~cm), 3.97~cm with $K=4$ and 4.94~cm with $K=3$ (P90 8.07~cm). Beyond $K=5$ the additional orientations do not reduce an error that is dominated by model bias; with $K=3$ the problem is exactly determined and no redundancy is left against these biases, which shows mainly in the tail of the distribution. The orientations need not be uniformly spaced either. Over all 252 subsets of five of the ten angles, most of them irregular, the mean error is 4.56~cm. It grows with the largest azimuthal gap of the set: 3.73~cm for the two uniform subsets (gap of $72^\circ$), 4.06 and 4.52~cm for gaps of $108^\circ$ and $144^\circ$, and 5.40~cm when half a turn is missing; the best irregular subset reaches 3.55~cm. A receiver that turns irregularly, or only partially, thus remains usable, provided that its orientations are known.

\subsection{Calibration Budget}
\label{subsec:res-budget}
Because the direction and amplitude of every position do not depend on $A$ and $m$, the error on D2 can be recomputed exactly for any calibration. Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}(b) does so for calibrations obtained from $N$ points of D1 with the LED position known. A regular $n\times n$ grid over the working area reaches the accuracy of the full map with very few points: 3.45, 3.54 and 3.56~cm for $2\times2$, $3\times3$ and $4\times4$, against 3.65~cm with all 19\,881 points (3.66~cm with the staged calibration of Table~\ref{tab:parameters}). Random points over the area need about ten to approach it (median 3.86~cm for $N=10$). Concentrating the points around the LED, which looks natural, does not work: a $3\times3$ grid with 0.1 or 0.2~m spacing centered on the LED gives 21.8 and 17.3~cm, and it recovers only when the spacing reaches 0.4~m (4.16~cm). The reason is the angle-dependent apparent order of Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}(a): points near the axis push $m$ upwards, and the calibration must sample the emission angles at which the system operates. No pair $(A,m)$ does better than 3.14~cm on D2, even when chosen with the ground truth; this is the floor of the single-order pattern model, reached within 0.5~cm by nine well-placed points.

\add{The stage zero can be calibrated with equally few positions. Fitted with the profile-consistency test on the nine positions of a $3\times3$ grid at a single height, instead of the 125 positions of D2, it lies between $66.2^\circ$ and $66.8^\circ$ for the five heights, and the mean error on the independent blind test D3 remains between 4.10 and 4.12~cm, against 4.10~cm with the value of Table~\ref{tab:parameters} (Section~\ref{subsec:res-blind}). With nine positions drawn at random it stays below 4.2~cm in 95\,\% of the draws, whereas five positions occasionally give up to 5.0~cm.}

\subsection{Blind Random Test}
\label{subsec:res-blind}
On the 131 random positions of D3, estimated live with $K=5$ and the calibration frozen beforehand, the mean 3D error is 4.10~cm (95\,\% bootstrap confidence interval 3.77--4.43~cm, median 3.91~cm, RMSE 4.53~cm, P90 6.73~cm, maximum 12.5~cm), with a mean horizontal error of 2.64~cm, a mean vertical error of $-1.96$~cm and a mean direction error of $0.90^\circ$ (Figs.~\ref{fig:cdf} and~\ref{fig:random}). Recomputing these estimates offline from the stored RSS reproduces the live values within 0.002~cm. The result is close to the $K=5$ subsets of D2 (3.73~cm)\del{; the slightly larger error and tail come from positions near the edges of the area and near the lowest height, which the regular grid samples less densely, and from the stage zero, which in D3 is no longer refitted}\add{. The error is slightly larger, and its tail longer, mainly near the border of the area (5.2~cm on average within 0.15~m of it, against 3.4~cm elsewhere), and the stage zero is not refitted on D3}. The accuracy measured on the regular grid thus transfers to unseen positions.

\subsection{The Other LEDs as Single-LED Systems}
\label{subsec:res-leds}
The 300, 700 and 900~kHz LEDs are recorded in every measurement and provide three more single-LED systems with the same receiver and positions. Each was calibrated \del{``from scratch'' }on D1 like the 500~kHz LED and used alone to \del{position the 125 sites}\add{estimate the 125 positions} of D2 (Table~\ref{tab:leds}). Two LED positions are compared: the center of the pattern, fitted on D1 together with $A$ and $m$, and the geometric position, obtained by intersecting the directions to that LED estimated at the known positions of D2, which do not depend on its gain or pattern. To avoid evaluating on the data used to locate the LED, the geometric position used at each height is triangulated from the other four.

The calibration budget holds for every LED: $3\times3$ and $4\times4$ grids give the same accuracy as the full map. The accuracies, however, differ widely, from about 3~cm (500~kHz) to 17--24~cm (700~kHz), and the difference between the two LED positions reveals why. For the 700~kHz LED the pattern center lies 17.9~cm from the geometric position, which corresponds to a tilt of about $5.5^\circ$ of its optical axis over the 1.865~m between the LED and the scan plane; the other LEDs show tilts of $0.8^\circ$ to $1.9^\circ$. The model assumes a vertical axis, so a tilted LED biases the calibrated pattern, and no number of calibration points can compensate for it. Using the geometric position improves every LED, but only a model with the LED tilt, or a realigned LED, would remove the bias. The triangulated LED heights, 2.95--2.97~m, are 3--5~cm below the tape value, consistent with the small negative vertical errors of the estimates. \del{Read in reverse, the method diagnoses the installation: it measures}\add{The method thus also measures} where each LED is and where it points, from the same data used for positioning.
