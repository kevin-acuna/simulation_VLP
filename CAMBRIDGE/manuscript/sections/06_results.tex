\subsection{Measurement Noise}
\label{subsec:res-noise}
Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum} shows the spectrum of a 100-ms record of D2: the four tones stand out over a flat floor, and the flicker of the room lights is confined to low frequencies. The noise of the RSS~\eqref{eq:lockin} was estimated from the same projection at off-tone frequencies, 20 to 40~kHz away from each tone, in the 1\,250 records of D2. Its standard deviation is $\sigma=1.92~\mu$V at 500~kHz; its 95th percentile over the records, which span all orientations and positions, is only 7\,\% higher (2.06~$\mu$V), which justifies a common value for all orientations, and 1.9--2.1~$\mu$V at the other tones. Over D2, the 500~kHz RSS ranges from 1.07 to 20.7~mV, so the SNR $s_i/\sigma$ lies between 573 and 10\,190 (median 4\,540). Consecutive records at the same pose, available for 47 poses, differ by a median relative standard deviation of 0.019\,\% (P90 0.037\,\%), as expected from this floor alone; within a record time, neither the LED nor the receiver chain adds noticeable fluctuations. Measurement noise is therefore small, and the question is whether it limits the accuracy (Section~\ref{subsec:res-bound}).

\subsection{LED Radiation Patterns}
\label{subsec:res-env}
Fig.~\ref{fig:maps} shows the flat-PD maps of D1 for the four LEDs. Each LED has a single smooth lobe, without visible reflections, and the joint fit of~\eqref{eq:led-calibration} locates the center of its pattern ($+$). For the 500~kHz LED this center is at $(281.1,904.8)$~mm, the value of Table~\ref{tab:parameters}.

The pattern does not follow a single cosine power, however. Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}(a) shows the apparent order of each point of D1, computed with $A$ fixed by the point closest to the axis. It decreases from about 11 near the axis to a stable value of 6.0 beyond $\phi\approx20^\circ$. The measured product $R(\phi)\cos^m\phi$ is thus more peaked near the axis than the model; the data constrain only this product, so the deviation may originate in the LED optics, in the response of the PD near normal incidence, or in a slight tilt of the LED. The staged calibration gives $m=6.09$, close to the value beyond $20^\circ$, where most positions of the working area are seen; a joint fit over the whole map gives $A=0.0611$~V\,m$^2$ and $m=5.79$.

\subsection{Receiver Response Model}
\label{subsec:res-model}
Table~\ref{tab:models} compares the three response families on D2. The shape test~\eqref{eq:shape} at the true positions does not involve $A$, $m$ or the LED pattern; it gives a residual of 0.80\,\% for SQ, 1.42\,\% for $\cos^{1.9}$ and 6.63\,\% for $\cos$, with the stage zero fitted for each model (with the nominal $65^\circ$: 0.88, 1.47 and 6.63\,\%). The positioning results follow the same order. The Lambertian receiver $\cos\psi$ underestimates the decay of the response with the incidence angle; to explain the measured contrast between orientations it places the LED at a much larger zenith angle (direction errors of $11^\circ$--$12^\circ$), and the range stage converts this into errors of 0.7--0.8~m, with 10 and 9 failures for GLS and WLS. The cosine order fitted to the same device~\cite{Bastiaens2020} reduces the error to 8.2~cm, still more than twice that of SQ, with a vertical bias of $-5.6$~cm. A model that fits amplitudes reasonably well can still misrepresent their slopes (Section~\ref{subsec:fim}), and the shape test exposes it.

With the SQ response, GLS and NLS agree within 0.01~cm, as expected at high SNR; WLS ignores the correlation between ratios that share a reference and is slightly worse. NLS is used in the following.

\subsection{Number of Orientations}

\subsection{Calibration Budget}
\label{subsec:res-budget}
Because the direction and amplitude of every position do not depend on $A$ and $m$, the error on D2 can be recomputed exactly for any calibration. Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}(b) does so for calibrations obtained from $N$ points of D1 with the LED position known. A regular $n\times n$ grid over the working area reaches the accuracy of the full map with very few points: 3.45, 3.54 and 3.57~cm for $2\times2$, $3\times3$ and $4\times4$, against 3.65~cm with all 19\,881 points. Random points over the area need about ten to approach it (median 3.86~cm for $N=10$). Concentrating the points around the LED, which looks natural, fails: a $3\times3$ grid with 0.1 or 0.2~m spacing centered on the LED gives 21.8 and 17.3~cm, and it recovers only when the spacing reaches 0.4~m (4.16~cm). The reason is the angle-dependent apparent order of Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}(a): points near the axis push $m$ upwards, and the calibration must sample the emission angles at which the system operates. No pair $(A,m)$ does better than 3.15~cm on D2, even when chosen with the ground truth; this is the floor of the single-order pattern model, reached within 0.5~cm by nine well-placed points.

\subsection{Blind Random Test}
\label{subsec:res-blind}
On the 131 random positions of D3, estimated live with $K=5$ and the calibration frozen beforehand, the mean 3D error is 4.10~cm (median 3.91~cm, RMSE 4.53~cm, P90 6.73~cm, maximum 12.5~cm), with a mean horizontal error of 2.64~cm, a mean vertical error of $-1.96$~cm and a mean direction error of $0.90^\circ$ (Figs.~\ref{fig:cdf} and~\ref{fig:random}). Recomputing these estimates offline from the stored RSS reproduces the live values within 0.002~cm. The result is close to the $K=5$ subsets of D2 (3.74~cm); the slightly larger error and tail come from positions near the edges of the area and near the lowest height, which the regular grid samples less densely, and from the stage zero, which in D3 is no longer refitted. The accuracy measured on the regular grid thus transfers to unseen positions.

\subsection{Integrity Monitoring}
\label{subsec:res-integrity}
During the first session of D3, the tilt axis of the stage moved by itself from the 48th position on, while it was not commanded; its encoder then reported up to $2.85^\circ$. Fig.~\ref{fig:integrity} shows the residual $\hat\rho$ of the fit at every position of that session. Its median rises from 0.42\,\% before the fault to 1.74\,\% after it, while the mean 3D error grows from 3.28 to 13.9~cm (maximum 25.1~cm). A threshold of 0.9\,\%, the largest residual observed on the fault-free grid D2, is exceeded by 82\,\% of the faulty positions but also by 11.5\,\% of the valid positions of D3, because five orientations give noisier residuals than ten. Requiring two consecutive exceedances removes all false alarms over the 131 valid positions and raises the alarm at the 51st position, four positions after the onset (three consecutive exceedances: 52nd). The residual therefore detects that the assumed orientations are wrong without any ground truth, which is why these 44 positions are excluded from D3. A later dense-grid campaign, in which the rotation stage slipped while its encoder kept reporting the commanded angles, was discarded for the same reason.

\subsection{The Other LEDs as Single-LED Systems}
\label{subsec:res-leds}
The 300, 700 and 900~kHz LEDs are recorded in every measurement and provide three more single-LED systems with the same receiver and positions. Each was calibrated ``from scratch'' on D1 like the 500~kHz LED and used alone to position the 125 sites of D2 (Table~\ref{tab:leds}). Two LED positions are compared: the center of the pattern, fitted on D1 together with $A$ and $m$, and the geometric position, obtained by intersecting the directions to that LED estimated at the known positions of D2, which do not depend on its gain or pattern. To avoid evaluating on the data used to locate the LED, the geometric position used at each height is triangulated from the other four.

The calibration budget holds for every LED: $3\times3$ and $4\times4$ grids give the same accuracy as the full map. The accuracies, however, differ widely, from about 3~cm (500~kHz) to 17--24~cm (700~kHz), and the difference between the two LED positions reveals why. For the 700~kHz LED the pattern center lies 17.9~cm from the geometric position, which corresponds to a tilt of about $5.5^\circ$ of its optical axis over the 1.865~m between the LED and the scan plane; the other LEDs show tilts of $0.8^\circ$ to $1.9^\circ$. The model assumes a vertical axis, so a tilted LED biases the calibrated pattern, and no number of calibration points can compensate for it. Using the geometric position improves every LED, but only a model with the LED tilt, or a realigned LED, would remove the bias. The triangulated LED heights, 2.95--2.97~m, are 3--5~cm below the tape value, consistent with the small negative vertical errors of the estimates. Read in reverse, the method diagnoses the installation: it measures where each LED is and where it points, from the same data used for positioning.

\subsection{Comparison With the Position Error Bound}
\label{subsec:res-bound}
Fig.~\ref{fig:bound} compares the measured errors on D2 with the bound of the testbed, evaluated at the same positions and orientations with the calibrated SQ model and the measured noise. With exactly known normals, the median bound is 0.75~mm for $K=10$ (P90 1.61~mm) and 1.06~mm for $K=5$; including the relative fluctuation of consecutive records raises it to 0.94~mm. The median measured error is about 39 times larger: the system is far from noise-limited. The bound with orientation uncertainty indicates what this gap is made of. An uncertainty of only $0.1^\circ$ per tangent component already raises the median bound to 4.1~mm, $0.5^\circ$ to 2.0~cm and $1^\circ$ to 4.0~cm; the measured errors lie between the last two curves. Small angular errors in the model thus explain errors of a few centimeters, and the measurements show several such sources: the stage zero, fitted per height, drifts by about $0.5^\circ$ over the ten hours of D2; the estimated directions carry the small elevation bias revealed by the triangulated LED heights; and the single-order pattern model alone has a floor of 3.15~cm (Section~\ref{subsec:res-budget}), which~\eqref{eq:range-sensitivity} links to direction errors of the order of one degree. Further accuracy will come from better models of the LED and of the receiver orientation, not from more averaging, power or orientations.

For the geometry of the testbed, the same bound also confirms the tilt chosen in Section~\ref{subsec:design}: with $K=5$ and $\sigma_\theta=1^\circ$, its median would decrease by only 27\,\% from $15^\circ$ to its minimum at $35^\circ$.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Orientation Errors}
\label{subsec:res-orient}
The bound attributes the gap between the noise and the measured errors to orientation errors of a fraction of a degree. Their effect on the estimators was measured directly on D2: the normals given to the estimators were perturbed by Gaussian errors with a standard deviation $\sigma_\theta$ per tangent component, either independent for each orientation, like the jitter of an encoder or an inertial sensor, or common to the ten orientations of a position, like an error of the angular zero or of the axis of the stage. The 125 positions were estimated again from the measured RSS, with 20 draws per error level.

Fig.~\ref{fig:orient}(a) shows the result. Errors up to $0.2^\circ$ are negligible: the mean 3D error of NLS grows from 3.66 to 3.79~cm with independent errors and to 3.84~cm with common ones. It reaches 4.37 and 4.80~cm at $0.5^\circ$, and 6.02 and 6.98~cm at $1^\circ$. A common error is the more harmful, because it rotates all the normals together and is not averaged over the orientations. GLS follows NLS within 0.15~cm; WLS starts higher (4.10~cm without perturbation), but the difference fades as the orientation errors dominate. No estimate failed.

Fig.~\ref{fig:orient}(b) isolates the effect of the perturbation through the RMS shift of the NLS estimates, and compares it with the PEB for the same orientation uncertainty~\eqref{eq:fim-orientation}, evaluated at the same positions. Both grow linearly with $\sigma_\theta$, by about 5.3~cm per degree for independent errors and 6.8~cm per degree for common ones. For common errors the shift coincides with the bound within 3\,\%: NLS is then efficient. For independent errors it lies 11--19\,\% above the bound, because NLS weights all orientations equally, whereas the bound weights each one by its effective variance~\eqref{eq:effective-variance}. In this testbed, the orientation must thus be known to about $0.2^\circ$ for its errors to remain small against the model bias, and an angular reference free of drift matters more than a low jitter.

\subsection{Observation Window}
\label{subsec:res-window}
All the RSS used so far integrate 100~ms of signal. To find how much signal is actually needed, the RSS of every D2 record was recomputed over shorter windows, from 10~$\mu$s to 100~ms, cut from the recorded waveforms. Each window is exactly what a shorter acquisition with the same sample rate and scale would have delivered, and up to ten disjoint segments of each record, spread over its 100~ms, give independent repetitions. The calibration of Table~\ref{tab:parameters} was kept with one correction: because of the frequency offsets of the tones (Section~\ref{subsec:hardware}), the projection at the nominal frequency loses 0.52\,\% of the 500~kHz amplitude over 100~ms but almost nothing over shorter windows, so $A$ was rescaled by this known coherent gain for each window length.

Fig.~\ref{fig:window}(a) shows the result. The mean 3D error is unchanged down to a window of 10~ms (3.67~cm), and rises only to 3.71~cm at 5~ms and 3.93~cm at 1~ms; below 0.1~ms it degrades quickly, to 9.5~cm at 10~$\mu$s. Below 10~ms the error also depends on where the window falls within the flicker period of the room lights (shaded range). Five orientations follow the same curve slightly higher (3.76, 3.81 and 4.21~cm at 10, 5 and 1~ms). A tenth of the signal recorded in this work is thus enough, and a hundredth costs about 3~mm.

Fig.~\ref{fig:window}(b) shows what sets this limit. When the segments are taken at a single phase of the 100~Hz flicker, one every 10~ms, the spread of the RSS follows the off-tone noise floor of Section~\ref{subsec:res-noise}, scaled as $T^{-1/2}$, over the whole range. Segments spread over the record, as an unsynchronized receiver would acquire them, show about twice that spread between 0.1 and 10~ms: within a flicker period, the amplitude measured over a short window changes with the instantaneous flicker level, by about 0.4\,\% rms at 500~kHz. The irregular shape of this dependence points to the 8-bit digitizer: its scale is set by the flicker, so that one quantization step (2.5~mV) is comparable to the tones themselves (1.1 to 20.7~mV). The excess vanishes once the window covers a whole flicker period, which is why 10~ms is the knee of both curves. A Hann window, which reduces spectral leakage, does not help (4.03~cm at 1~ms): the limit is not leakage of the flicker into the tone bin.

The minimum window thus depends on the link, through three mechanisms. The noise term scales as $\sigma/(s_i\sqrt T)$, so the window required for a given accuracy grows as the inverse square of the SNR: a link with half the SNR needs four times the window. The flicker term depends on the ambient light and on the digitizer: windows of a whole number of flicker periods (10~ms with 50~Hz mains, 8.3~ms with 60~Hz) average it out, and a finer digitizer or a steadier ambient light would move the knee towards the noise limit. Finally, longer windows do not pay off: the noise is already negligible at 10~ms, while the coherent loss of the nominal projection grows as $(\pi\Delta f\,T)^2/6$ for a frequency offset $\Delta f$, reaching 1.5\,\% for the 900~kHz tone at 100~ms. Beyond a few hundred milliseconds the tone frequencies would have to be tracked, and in any case the calibration and the positioning must use the same window, or its coherent gain must be corrected as done here.

\subsection{Acquisition and Processing Time}
\label{subsec:res-time}
A position of D2 ($K=10$) took 4.2~min on average, and a position of the live test ($K=5$) 3.7~min. About 87\,\% of this time is the rotation of the stage at $1.5^\circ$/s; each record, including its transfer, takes about one second. The signal itself is not the bottleneck either, since 10~ms per orientation suffice (Section~\ref{subsec:res-window}). The duration is a property of the actuator, not of the method: a stage rotating at about $20^\circ$/s would bring a five-orientation position below one minute.

Processing is faster still. Table~\ref{tab:proc} gives the computation time once the $K$ records are available, measured in MATLAB on the laptop that ran the experiments, with all its threads and with a single one. Extracting the amplitude of the tone from a 100-ms record, including the conversion of the raw samples to volts, takes 5.3~ms by projection at the tone frequency (7.6~ms on one thread); a full FFT, which delivers all the tones at once, takes 7.0~ms (8.7~ms). A 10-ms record needs less than 1~ms. Each estimator then takes about 2~ms per position, with a 95th percentile below 4~ms, for ten as for five orientations. With the SQ response, GLS and WLS are iterative as well (Section~\ref{subsec:estimator}), so the three methods cost the same and the choice among them can rest on accuracy alone (Table~\ref{tab:models}). A complete position with $K=10$ thus takes about 80~ms of computation on a single thread with 100-ms records, and about 7~ms with 10-ms records, against minutes of acquisition: computation does not limit the method. Learning-based estimators can also be evaluated quickly once trained, but they require a training database recorded over the working area and a training stage, and must be retrained when the installation changes; the estimators used here evaluate a physical model whose parameters come from the calibration of Section~\ref{subsec:led-calibration}.
