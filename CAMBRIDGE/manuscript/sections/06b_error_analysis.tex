This section examines what limits the accuracy, how errors of the orientation affect it, and how much signal and computation the system needs. It ends with the limitations of the study.

\subsection{Comparison With the Position Error Bound}
\label{subsec:res-bound}
Fig.~\ref{fig:bound} compares the measured errors on D2 with the bound of the testbed, evaluated at the same positions and orientations with the calibrated SQ model and the measured noise. With exactly known normals, the median bound is 1.26~mm for $K=10$ (P90 2.69~mm) and 1.78~mm for $K=5$. At each position the measured error is, in median, about 23 times the bound: the system is far from noise-limited. The bound with orientation uncertainty indicates what this gap is made of. An uncertainty of only $0.1^\circ$ per tangent component already raises the median bound to 4.3~mm, $0.5^\circ$ to 2.0~cm and $1^\circ$ to 4.0~cm. The measured errors lie between the last two curves. Small angular errors in the model thus explain errors of a few centimeters, and the measurements show two such sources. The estimated directions converge 3--5~cm below the \del{tape-measured}\add{measured} height of the LEDs (Section~\ref{subsec:res-leds}), a fraction of a degree in elevation, and the single-order pattern model alone has a floor of 3.14~cm (Section~\ref{subsec:res-budget}), which, through~\eqref{eq:range-sensitivity}, corresponds to direction errors of the order of one degree. Further accuracy will come from better models of the LED and of the receiver orientation, not from a longer observation, more power or more orientations.

The design rules of Section~\ref{subsec:design} can also be checked on the testbed. Its LED is narrower than that of the nominal scenario ($\Phi_{1/2}=26.8^\circ$ for $m=6.09$), but it is seen at no more than $37.6^\circ$ from the vertical, so that every orientation keeps it in view up to a tilt of about $27^\circ$. With $K=5$, the bound stays below 10~cm at every position of D2 from $\theta=8.5^\circ$ for $\sigma_\theta=0.5^\circ$, and from $18.5^\circ$ for $\sigma_\theta=1^\circ$. At $15^\circ$, 95\,\% of the positions are still covered in the latter case, and all of them with $K=10$. The tilt of $15^\circ$ thus lies inside the window of the testbed for orientation errors of a fraction of a degree, and a larger tilt would gain little: with $K=5$ and $\sigma_\theta=1^\circ$, the median bound would decrease by only 27\,\% from $15^\circ$ to its minimum at $35^\circ$.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Orientation Errors}
\label{subsec:res-orient}
According to the bound, orientation errors of a fraction of a degree are enough to explain the gap between the noise and the measured errors. Their effect on the estimators was measured directly on D2: the normals given to the estimators were perturbed by Gaussian errors with a standard deviation $\sigma_\theta$ per tangent component, either independent for each orientation, like the jitter of an encoder or an inertial sensor, or common to the ten orientations of a position, like an error of the stage zero or of the axis of the stage. The 125 positions were estimated again from the measured RSS, with 20 draws per error level.

Fig.~\ref{fig:orient}(a) shows the result. Errors up to $0.2^\circ$ are negligible: the mean \mbox{3-D} error of NLS grows from 3.66 to 3.78~cm with independent errors and to 3.84~cm with common ones. It reaches 4.37 and 4.79~cm at $0.5^\circ$, and 6.01 and 6.98~cm at $1^\circ$. A common error is the more harmful, because it rotates all the normals together and is not averaged over the orientations. GLS follows NLS within 0.15~cm. WLS starts higher (4.10~cm without perturbation), but the difference fades as the orientation errors dominate.

Fig.~\ref{fig:orient}(b) isolates the effect of the perturbation through the RMS shift of the NLS estimates, and compares it with the PEB for the same orientation uncertainty~\eqref{eq:fim-orientation}, evaluated at the same positions. Both grow linearly with $\sigma_\theta$, by about 5.3~cm per degree for independent errors and 6.8~cm per degree for common ones. For common errors the shift coincides with the bound within 4\,\%: NLS is then efficient. For independent errors it lies 5--19\,\% above the bound, because NLS weights all orientations equally, whereas the bound weights each one by its effective variance~\eqref{eq:effective-variance}. In this testbed, the orientation must thus be known to about $0.2^\circ$ for its errors to remain small against the model bias, and an angular reference free of drift matters more than a low jitter. For an IMU, whose attitude error changes little during one rotation, the common case applies.

\subsection{Observation Window}
\label{subsec:res-window}
All the RSS used so far integrate 100~ms of signal. To find how much signal is actually needed, the RSS of every D2 record was recomputed over shorter windows, from 10~$\mu$s to 100~ms, cut from the recorded waveforms. Each window is exactly what a shorter acquisition with the same sample rate and scale would have delivered, and up to ten disjoint segments of each record, spread over its 100~ms, give independent repetitions. As a system acquiring shorter windows would be calibrated with them, the LED calibration of Section~\ref{subsec:led-calibration} is made, for each window length, on the D1 records processed with the same window, and D2 is evaluated with that calibration.

Fig.~\ref{fig:window} shows the result. The mean \mbox{3-D} error is unchanged down to a window of 10~ms (3.67~cm), and rises only to 3.69~cm at 5~ms and 3.81~cm at 1~ms. Below 0.1~ms it degrades quickly, to 9.2~cm at 10~$\mu$s. Below 10~ms it also varies between the segments of a record (shaded range). Five orientations follow the same curve slightly higher (3.75, 3.79 and 4.11~cm at 10, 5 and 1~ms). A tenth of the signal recorded in this work is thus enough, and a hundredth costs about 1.5~mm.

\subsection{Acquisition and Processing Time}
\label{subsec:res-time}
A position of D2 ($K=10$) took 4.2~min on average, and a position of the live test ($K=5$) 3.7~min. About 87\,\% of this time is the rotation of the stage at $1.5^\circ$/s, and each record, including its transfer, takes about one second. The signal itself is not the bottleneck, since 10~ms per orientation suffice (Section~\ref{subsec:res-window}). The duration is thus set by the rotation stage of the testbed, not by the method: a stage rotating at about $20^\circ$/s would already bring a five-orientation position below one minute, and faster rotating platforms are discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:discussion}. The short window also eases an integration with communication. A tone observed for about 10~ms per orientation could be embedded as a pilot in the frames of an optical wireless link from the same LED, so that positioning and communication share the transmitter and the receiver, as in integrated sensing and communication (ISAC)~\cite{Ma2022}. This integration is left for future work.

Processing is faster still. In MATLAB, on a single thread of the laptop that ran the experiments (AMD Ryzen~9 8940HX), the projection~\eqref{eq:lockin} takes 7.6~ms for a 100-ms record and 0.5~ms for a 10-ms record, and the NLS estimate about 2~ms per position. A complete position with $K=10$ thus takes about 80~ms of computation with 100-ms records, and about 7~ms with 10-ms records, against minutes of acquisition: computation does not limit the method.
