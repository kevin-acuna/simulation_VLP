This section examines what limits the accuracy, how errors of the orientation affect it, and how much signal and computation the system needs. It ends with the limitations of the study.

\subsection{Comparison With the Position Error Bound}
\label{subsec:res-bound}
Fig.~\ref{fig:bound} compares the measured errors on D2 with the bound of the testbed, evaluated at the same positions and orientations with the calibrated SQ model and the measured noise. With exactly known normals, the median bound is 1.26~mm for $K=10$, more than twenty times smaller than the measured errors: the system is far from noise-limited. The bound with orientation uncertainty indicates what this gap is made of. An uncertainty of $0.5^\circ$ per tangent component raises the median bound to 2.0~cm and $1^\circ$ to 4.0~cm, and the measured errors lie between these two curves. Model errors equivalent to orientation errors of less than one degree are thus enough to explain the measured errors, and two of them were identified. The estimated directions converge 3--5~cm below the measured height of the LEDs (Section~\ref{subsec:res-leds}), and the single-order pattern model alone has a floor of 3.14~cm (Section~\ref{subsec:res-budget}). Further accuracy will come from better models of the LED and of the receiver orientation, not from a longer observation, more power or more orientations.

The design rules of Section~\ref{subsec:design} also hold on the testbed. Its LED is seen at no more than $38^\circ$ from the vertical, so that every orientation keeps it in view up to a tilt of about $27^\circ$, and at the tilt of $15^\circ$, with $K=5$ and $\sigma_\theta=0.5^\circ$, the bound stays below 10~cm at every position of D2.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Orientation Errors}
\label{subsec:res-orient}
According to the bound, orientation errors of a fraction of a degree are enough to explain the gap between the noise and the measured errors. Their effect on the estimators was measured directly on D2: the normals given to the estimators were perturbed by Gaussian errors with a standard deviation $\sigma_\theta$ per tangent component, either independent for each orientation, like the jitter of an encoder or an inertial sensor, or common to the ten orientations of a position, like an error of the stage zero or of the axis of the stage. The 125 positions were estimated again from the measured RSS, with 20 draws per error level.

Fig.~\ref{fig:orient}(a) shows the result. Errors up to $0.2^\circ$ are negligible: the mean \mbox{3-D} error of NLS grows from 3.66 to less than 3.9~cm. At $1^\circ$, it reaches 6.0~cm with independent errors and 7.0~cm with common ones. A common error is the more harmful, because it rotates all the normals together and is not averaged over the orientations. GLS and WLS follow the same trend.

Fig.~\ref{fig:orient}(b) isolates the effect of the perturbation through the RMS shift of the NLS estimates, and compares it with the PEB for the same orientation uncertainty~\eqref{eq:fim-orientation}, evaluated at the same positions. Both grow linearly with $\sigma_\theta$, by about 5.3~cm per degree for independent errors and 6.8~cm per degree for common ones. For common errors the shift coincides with the bound: NLS is then efficient. For independent errors it lies above the bound, because NLS weights all orientations equally, whereas the bound weights each one by its effective variance~\eqref{eq:effective-variance}. In this testbed, the orientation must thus be known to about $0.2^\circ$ for its errors to remain small against the model bias, and an angular reference free of drift matters more than a low jitter. For an IMU, whose attitude error changes little during one rotation, the common case applies.

\subsection{Observation Window}
\label{subsec:res-window}
All the RSS used so far integrate 100~ms of signal. To find how much signal is actually needed, the RSS of every D2 record was recomputed over shorter windows, from 10~$\mu$s to 100~ms, cut from the recorded waveforms. Up to ten disjoint segments of each record give independent repetitions. For each window length, the LED is calibrated as in Section~\ref{subsec:led-calibration} on the D1 records processed with the same window.

Fig.~\ref{fig:window} shows the result. The mean \mbox{3-D} error is unchanged down to a window of 10~ms (3.67~cm), and rises only to 3.81~cm at 1~ms. Below 0.1~ms it degrades quickly, to 9.2~cm at 10~$\mu$s. Five orientations follow the same curve slightly higher. A tenth of the signal recorded in this work is thus enough.

\subsection{Acquisition and Processing Time}
\label{subsec:res-time}
A position of D2 ($K=10$) took 4.2~min on average, and a position of the live test ($K=5$) 3.7~min. Most of this time is the rotation of the stage at $1.5^\circ$/s. The signal itself is not the bottleneck, since 10~ms per orientation suffice (Section~\ref{subsec:res-window}). The duration is thus set by the rotation stage of the testbed, not by the method (Section~\ref{subsec:discussion}). The short window also eases an integration with communication. A tone observed for about 10~ms per orientation could be embedded as a pilot in the frames of an optical wireless link from the same LED, as in integrated sensing and communication~\cite{Ma2022}.

Processing is faster still. In MATLAB, on a single thread of a laptop, the projection~\eqref{eq:lockin} of the records and the NLS estimate of a position with $K=10$ take about 80~ms with 100-ms records and about 7~ms with 10-ms records, so that computation does not limit the method.
