\subsection{System Geometry}
\label{subsec:geometry}
A single LED is installed at a known position $\vect t\in\R^3$ with unit optical axis $\vect n_t$, and a single photodiode (PD) is placed at an unknown position $\vect r=[x,y,z]\trans$. While its optical center remains stationary, the PD is reoriented to $K$ known unit normals $\vect n_i\in\mathbb S^2$, $i=1,\ldots,K$, expressed in the same global frame as $\vect t$, and one received-signal-strength (RSS) measurement is taken at each orientation (Fig.~\ref{fig:system}). All three coordinates of $\vect r$ are unknown\add{, including the height of the receiver, which is not assumed to lie on a known plane}. \add{The global frame is fixed to the room, with its $Z$ axis pointing up.}

The LED--PD distance and the unit direction from the PD toward the LED are:
\begin{equation}
 d=\norm{\vect t-\vect r},\qquad
 \vect u=\frac{\vect t-\vect r}{d}.
 \label{eq:geometry}
\end{equation}
The irradiance angle $\phi$ at the LED and the incidence angle $\psi_i$ at the PD follow from:
\begin{equation}
 \cos\phi=-\vect n_t\trans\vect u,\qquad
 \cos\psi_i=\vect n_i\trans\vect u.
 \label{eq:incidence}
\end{equation}
Reorienting the receiver therefore changes only $\psi_i$; the distance $d$ and the irradiance angle $\phi$ are common to the $K$ measurements of a position. The inclination of $\vect n_i$ with respect to the vertical must not be confused with $\psi_i$, and no particular orientation pattern is assumed in this section. \add{The $K$ normals may be realized sequentially by a single reoriented PD, as in this work, or simultaneously by several PDs. With a single detector, the $K$ measurements share the same response, gain and acquisition chain, so that no matching between detectors is required.}

\subsection{Received-Signal Model}
\label{subsec:radiometry}
Under line-of-sight (LOS) propagation and a Lambertian point source~\cite{Kahn1997,Komine2004}, the LED, emitting the optical power $P_t$, delivers through the direct path the noiseless power $P_tH_i$ to the PD at orientation $i$, with the channel gain:
\begin{equation}
 H_i(\vect r)=\frac{(m+1)A_{\mathrm{PD}}}{2\pi d^2}\cos^{m}(\phi)\,T_s(\psi_i)\,g(\psi_i)\,R(\psi_i),
 \label{eq:channel}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\mathrm{PD}}$ is the active area of the detector, $m=-\ln2/\ln(\cos\Phi_{1/2})$ the Lambertian order of the LED with half-power semi-angle $\Phi_{1/2}$, $T_s(\psi)$ and $g(\psi)$ the gains of an optical filter and of an optical concentrator~\cite{Kahn1997}, and $R(\psi)$ the normalized angular response of the receiver introduced below. The model holds for $0\le\phi<\pi/2$ and $R(\psi_i)>0$; otherwise $H_i=0$. Reflections are neglected, and noise is introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:observations}. The receiver of this work has neither a filter nor a concentrator, so $T_s(\psi)=g(\psi)=1$ in the following; a constant transmission would in any case be absorbed by the calibrated gain introduced below.

The LED is intensity-modulated by a sinusoidal tone of frequency $f$, so that its optical power is $P_t(t)=\bar P_t+P_f\cos(2\pi ft)$, where $P_f$ is the amplitude of the modulated component. The PD converts the collected power into a photocurrent with responsivity $\gamma$ (in A/W), and its amplifier converts this current into a voltage with transimpedance gain $G_{\mathrm T}(f)$ (in V/A) at the tone frequency~\cite{Komine2004}. The quantity actually measured is the RMS amplitude of the tone in this voltage, extracted by a lock-in projection (Section~\ref{subsec:observations}). Its noiseless value at orientation $i$, the signal $s_i$, is:
\begin{equation}
 s_i(\vect r)=\frac{\gamma\,G_{\mathrm T}(f)\,P_f}{\sqrt2}\,H_i(\vect r)
 =\frac{A\cos^{m}(\phi)}{d^2}\,R(\psi_i),
 \label{eq:rss-model}
\end{equation}
with the lumped gain $A$, in $\mathrm{V\,m^2}$:
\begin{equation}
 A=\frac{(m+1)A_{\mathrm{PD}}\,\gamma\,G_{\mathrm T}(f)\,P_f}{2\sqrt2\,\pi}.
 \label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
Each factor of~\eqref{eq:gain} could be measured separately, but this would require several dedicated calibrations of the transmitter and of the receiver chain. Grouping them into $A$ reduces this effort to a single joint calibration of $A$ and $m$ from RSS measurements at known positions (Section~\ref{subsec:led-calibration}), which also absorbs any gain of the receiver chain that is not modelled explicitly.

Model~\eqref{eq:rss-model} has a separable structure that the rest of the paper exploits:
\begin{equation}
 s_i(\vect r)=\beta(\vect r)\,R(\psi_i),\qquad
 \beta(\vect r)=\frac{A\cos^{m}(\phi)}{d^2}.
 \label{eq:separable}
\end{equation}
The shape of the $K$ signals, $R(\psi_i)$ up to a common factor, depends only on the direction $\vect u$, through $\cos\psi_i=\vect n_i\trans\vect u$; the common amplitude $\beta$ carries the distance. The LED gain and radiation pattern enter only through $\beta$. When the dependence on $\vect u$ must be explicit, we write $R(\vect n_i\trans\vect u)$ for the response at the incidence angle $\arccos(\vect n_i\trans\vect u)$.

\subsection{Receiver Angular Response}
\label{subsec:response}
The dimensionless function $R(\psi)$, with $R(0)=1$, is the effective angular response of the receiver, including the projected-area effect; no additional $\cos\psi$ factor multiplies it. It is assumed axisymmetric. The conventional choice is $R(\psi)=\cos\psi$~\cite{Kahn1997,Wang2024}, and the generalized form $R(\psi)=\cos^{m_R}\psi$ is also common. These forms are adopted mostly for analytical convenience: measurements of commercial photodiodes show that their effective response can depart markedly from a cosine, and that ignoring the mismatch degrades RSS-based positioning~\cite{Bastiaens2020}. The method studied here depends on the response even more directly, because the direction is inferred from the variation of $R$ over the orientations. The formulation therefore keeps $R(\psi)$ general. As an analytical alternative to the cosine, we consider the SQ model, which described the two commercial PDs characterized in~\cite{Bastiaens2020} better than cosine models:
\begin{equation}
 R_{\mathrm{SQ}}(\psi)=
 \left[1-a\,\psi^2\right]_+,\qquad
 a=\frac{1-1/\sqrt2}{\psi_{3\mathrm{dB}}^2},
 \label{eq:sq-response}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot]_+=\max(\cdot,0)$ and $\psi_{3\mathrm{dB}}$ is the angle at which the response equals $1/\sqrt2$. For the Thorlabs PDA100A2 used here, $\psi_{3\mathrm{dB}}=0.61$~rad, so that the response vanishes at $\psi=a^{-1/2}=64.6^\circ$, and the fitted cosine order is $m_R=1.9$~\cite{Bastiaens2020}. The three families are compared on our data in Section~\ref{subsec:res-model}; none of them is assumed to be the true response a priori.

\subsection{RSS Acquisition and Observation Statistics}
\label{subsec:observations}
The PD output $v[n]$ is digitized at rate $f_s$ over a record of $N$ samples (duration $T=N/f_s$) at each orientation, after the receiver has settled. The RSS is the RMS amplitude of the discrete Fourier coefficient at the tone frequency $f$:
\begin{equation}
 y_i=\frac{\sqrt2}{N}\left|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}v[n]\,e^{-j2\pi fn/f_s}\right|.
 \label{eq:lockin}
\end{equation}
When $fT$ is an integer, this projection \del{rejects signals at other frequencies, such as}\add{rejects the other tones that also complete an integer number of cycles, and strongly attenuates slow components such as} the low-frequency flicker of the ambient light, and its noise is set by the noise spectral density in a band of width $1/T$ around $f$. At the signal-to-noise ratios of this work (Section~\ref{subsec:res-noise}), the measurement is well modelled as:
\begin{equation}
 y_i=s_i(\vect r)+\epsilon_i,\qquad
 \epsilon_i\sim\mathcal N\!\left(0,\sigma_i^2/L\right),
 \label{eq:observation}
\end{equation}
with independent errors across orientations, where $\sigma_i$ is the standard deviation of one record at orientation $i$ and $L$ the number of records averaged per orientation ($L=1$ throughout the experiments; $N$ remains the number of samples of a record). In general $\sigma_i$ depends on the orientation, through the shot noise of the collected signal and ambient light~\cite{Komine2004}, and the formulation keeps it so. When the ambient light and the receiver electronics dominate, the noise becomes nearly independent of the signal~\cite{Kahn1997}; this is the case of our receiver (Section~\ref{subsec:res-noise}), and the experiments use a common value $\sigma$. The orientation-wise SNR is $\mathrm{SNR}_i=s_i/\sigma_i$. In this baseline the actual normals $\vect n_i$ are known; orientation errors are treated in Section~\ref{subsec:orientation-uncertainty}. Background, gain drift and deviations from the LOS model are not averaged out by repeated records and are discussed with the experimental results.
