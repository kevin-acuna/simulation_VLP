\subsection{Fisher Information}
\label{subsec:fim}
Under~\eqref{eq:observation}, the Fisher information matrix (FIM) of the position is~\cite{Kay1993}:
\begin{equation}
 \vect I(\vect r)=\vect J\trans\vect W\vect J,\qquad
 {\vect W=\diag\!\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2},\ldots,\frac{1}{\sigma_K^2}\right)},
 \label{eq:fim}
\end{equation}
where the rows of $\vect J\in\R^{K\times3}$ are the gradients $\nabla s_i\trans$, and the position error bound (PEB), the lower bound on the RMS position error of any unbiased estimator, is $\mathrm{PEB}=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}\vect I^{-1}}$. For a common noise level $\sigma$, $\vect W=\vect I_K/\sigma^2$. With $\vect P_{\vect u}=\vect I_3-\vect u\vect u\trans$, $\nabla d=-\vect u$ and $\partial\vect u/\partial\vect r=-\vect P_{\vect u}/d$, the separable form~\eqref{eq:separable} gives:
\begin{equation}
 \nabla s_i=R(\psi_i)\nabla\beta-\frac{\beta}{d}\dot R(\psi_i)\left(\vect n_i-\cos\psi_i\,\vect u\right),
 \label{eq:gradient}
\end{equation}
where $\dot R(\psi)=\mathrm dR/\mathrm d(\cos\psi)$ is the derivative of the response with respect to the incidence cosine and, for the Lambertian pattern:
\begin{equation}
 \nabla\beta=\frac{\beta}{d}\left[(m+2)\,\vect u-\frac{m}{\cos\phi}\,(-\vect n_t)\right].
 \label{eq:beta-gradient}
\end{equation}
The first term of~\eqref{eq:gradient} is common to all orientations and carries range information, whereas the second depends on the orientation and carries direction information, in proportion to the slope $\dot R$ of the response and not to its value. For the SQ response, $\dot R(\psi)=2a\psi/\sin\psi$ inside its support, with $\dot R(0)=2a$.

\subsection{Local Identifiability}
\label{subsec:identifiability}
A displacement of the PD changes the signals only through the amplitude $\beta$ and the direction $\vect u$. Let $\vect E\in\R^{3\times2}$ be an orthonormal basis of the plane orthogonal to $\vect u$, and $\bm\omega$ the two coordinates of a small change of $\vect u$ in this basis. From~\eqref{eq:separable}, $\partial s_i/\partial\ln\beta=\beta R(\psi_i)$ and $\partial s_i/\partial\bm\omega\trans=\beta\dot R(\psi_i)\,\vect n_i\trans\vect E$. Wherever the LED illuminates the PD, the map from $\vect r$ to $(\ln\beta,\bm\omega)$ is invertible, so that $\vect J$ has the rank of the matrix of these derivatives:
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{rank}\vect J=\operatorname{rank}\left[\,\vect R,\ \diag(\dot{\vect R})\,\vect N\trans\vect E\,\right],
 \label{eq:rank}
\end{equation}
with $\vect R=[R(\psi_1),\ldots,R(\psi_K)]\trans$, $\dot{\vect R}$ defined likewise and $\vect N=[\vect n_1,\ldots,\vect n_K]$. The position is locally identifiable if this $K\times3$ matrix has rank three. Three consequences follow for the design of the system.

First, at least three illuminated orientations are needed, but they are not sufficient: the tangent projections $\vect E\trans\vect n_i$, weighted by the response slope, must span the plane orthogonal to $\vect u$. If the PD normal coincides with the rotation axis, all incidence angles are equal and~\eqref{eq:rank} has rank one; turning an untilted PD provides no information. The receiver must be tilted with respect to the rotation axis. For a cosine-power response,~\eqref{eq:rank} reduces to the rank of the illuminated normals; for the SQ response this reduction does not hold, and~\eqref{eq:rank} must be checked directly. Local identifiability does not imply global uniqueness either: with coplanar normals, the SQ model can admit two symmetric solutions with a finite bound, which is why the estimator of Section~\ref{subsec:estimator} uses several starting points.

Second, the direction needs neither $A$ nor the LED pattern, which only scale the first column of~\eqref{eq:rank}. The distance, in contrast, is only available through $\beta$: if $A$ were unknown, $\beta$ and $d$ could not be separated, and reorientation alone cannot remove this distance-gain ambiguity. The calibration of $A$ and $m$ is therefore a condition for three-dimensional positioning.

Third, the information depends on the response slope at the incidence angles actually visited, which links the tilt of the receiver to the attainable accuracy.

\subsection{Role of the Receiver Tilt}
\label{subsec:tilt}
The axial case makes this link explicit. Consider a vertical LED, the PD on its axis at distance $d$, and $K\geq3$ normals uniformly spaced in azimuth on a cone of half-angle $\theta$ about the vertical, all illuminated, with a common noise level $\sigma$. The FIM is diagonal (Appendix~\ref{app:axial}):
\begin{equation}
 \vect I=\frac{KA^2}{\sigma^2d^6}\diag\!\left(\frac{\dot R^2\sin^2\theta}{2},\ \frac{\dot R^2\sin^2\theta}{2},\ 4R^2\right),
 \label{eq:axial-fim}
\end{equation}
with $R$ and $\dot R$ evaluated at $\psi=\theta$, and the bound is:
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{PEB}^2=\frac{\sigma^2d^6}{KA^2}\left[\frac{4}{\dot R(\theta)^2\sin^2\theta}+\frac{1}{4R(\theta)^2}\right].
 \label{eq:axial-peb}
\end{equation}
The horizontal terms decrease with $\sin\theta$ and with the response slope, and they diverge as $\theta\to0$; the vertical term is controlled by the response value, i.e., by the received amplitude. The bound grows as $d^3$, and repeating independent orientations improves it as $1/\sqrt{K}$ only when the record duration per orientation is fixed.

\subsection{Orientation Uncertainty}
\label{subsec:orientation-uncertainty}
The normals are never known exactly. They are reported by a sensor, such as an encoder of the actuator or an IMU, with small angular errors that stay constant during the acquisition at each orientation and are modelled as zero-mean Gaussian with covariance $\bm\Sigma_\theta$. The true normals then become additional unknowns, described by a vector $\bm\eta$ of small rotations, and the sensor readings are noisy measurements of $\bm\eta$. The bound on the position follows from the joint FIM of $\vect r$ and $\bm\eta$, from which the nuisance parameter $\bm\eta$ is eliminated~\cite{Kay1993,Shen2022}. With $\vect D=\partial\vect s/\partial\bm\eta\trans$ the sensitivity of the signals $\vect s=[s_1,\ldots,s_K]\trans$ to the orientation, the resulting effective FIM is (Appendix~\ref{app:orientation}):
\begin{equation}
 {\vect I_e=\vect J\trans\left(\vect W^{-1}+\vect D\bm\Sigma_\theta\vect D\trans\right)^{-1}\vect J.}
 \label{eq:fim-orientation}
\end{equation}
Compared with~\eqref{eq:fim}, the covariance $\vect W^{-1}$ of the RSS is increased by $\vect D\bm\Sigma_\theta\vect D\trans$: orientation errors act as an additional noise on the RSS.

Two error structures are considered. In the first, the errors are independent between orientations, with a standard deviation $\sigma_\theta$ per tangent component, i.e., along each of the two directions in which a normal can turn. The added covariance is then diagonal, and~\eqref{eq:fim-orientation} amounts to replacing the noise variance of each orientation by:
\begin{equation}
 {\sigma_{i,\mathrm{eff}}^2=\sigma_i^2}+\sigma_\theta^2\,\beta^2\dot R(\psi_i)^2\sin^2\psi_i.
 \label{eq:effective-variance}
\end{equation}
The added term is the variance $\sigma_\theta^2$ of the incidence angle multiplied by the squared sensitivity of the signal to it, since $\partial s_i/\partial\psi_i=-\beta\dot R(\psi_i)\sin\psi_i$. Orientation errors thus behave as signal-proportional noise that a longer record does not reduce. In the second structure, a single error is common to all orientations, such as an error of the angular reference of the actuator or of a rigid mount: all the normals turn by the same small rotation, with $\bm\Sigma_\theta=\sigma_\theta^2\vect I_3$, and the added covariance is no longer diagonal, because the same error affects the $K$ signals. Appendix~\ref{app:orientation} gives $\vect D$ for both structures. At the high SNR of an amplified PD, orientation errors dominate the bound, as shown next.

\subsection{Numerical Study of the Bound}
\label{subsec:design}
The bound is now evaluated numerically, to see how the tilt, the number of orientations and the link budget interact, and to draw from it rules for the design of a receiver. The study uses the nominal indoor scenario of Table~\ref{tab:scenario}, which is independent of the testbed of Section~\ref{sec:testbed}. The response of the PD is the SQ model~\eqref{eq:sq-response} with the parameter published for a commercial amplified PD~\cite{Bastiaens2020}. The link budget is given by a single number, the SNR of an untilted PD on the floor below the LED, because the transmitted power, the area of the PD and the noise level enter the bound only through the ratio $A/\sigma$. The orientations are uniformly spaced in azimuth on a cone of tilt $\theta$, with independent errors (Section~\ref{subsec:orientation-uncertainty}), and the coverage is the fraction of positions at which the bound meets the criterion of the table. Fig.~\ref{fig:sim} summarizes the results.

With exactly known normals ($\sigma_\theta=0$), the tilt is not critical: for $K=5$, every position is covered from $\theta=5^\circ$, and the median bound at $15^\circ$ is 1.06~cm (Fig.~\ref{fig:sim}(a)). Orientation errors change this picture. Their contribution to~\eqref{eq:effective-variance} grows with the signal, so they set a floor that neither power nor averaging can lower, and the tilt becomes the main design variable. With $\sigma_\theta=1^\circ$, the median bound at $15^\circ$ rises to 5.7~cm, and covering every position with $K=5$ requires $\theta\ge14.5^\circ$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:sim}(b)). More orientations relax this requirement, down to $10^\circ$ for $K=10$, whereas three orientations never cover more than 98.5\,\% of the positions. The required tilt also follows the uncertainty: $8.5^\circ$ suffice for $K=5$ with $\sigma_\theta=0.5^\circ$, and with $\sigma_\theta=2^\circ$ no tilt covers the volume with $K\le10$. The orientation uncertainty thus sets the smallest admissible tilt.

The field of view limits the tilt from above. On a cone of tilt $\theta$, the largest incidence angle is close to $\phi_{\max}+\theta$, so every orientation sees the LED from every position only while this angle stays inside the support of the response, that is, up to $\theta\approx64.6^\circ-49.7^\circ\approx15^\circ$; at $30^\circ$, one position in ten has an orientation that misses the LED (Fig.~\ref{fig:sim}(b)). The bound tolerates such losses, and with $K=5$ the coverage stays above 98\,\% up to $45^\circ$, but the estimator then depends on the response near its cutoff, where the signal is weakest and the model is not differentiable. An aperture narrower than the support lowers this limit by the same amount. Larger tilts gain little in exchange: with $\sigma_\theta=1^\circ$, the median bound reaches its minimum at about $30^\circ$, only 26\,\% below its value at $15^\circ$.

The other parameters of the link matter mostly through the orientation errors. A narrow beam makes the received amplitude depend strongly on the direction, so that direction errors leak into the distance, as quantified in~\eqref{eq:range-sensitivity}: at $\theta=15^\circ$ and with $\sigma_\theta=1^\circ$, every position is covered only for $\Phi_{1/2}\ge40^\circ$, against $22.5^\circ$ with exactly known normals (Fig.~\ref{fig:sim}(c)). The SNR, finally, helps only up to a point (Fig.~\ref{fig:sim}(d)). With exactly known normals, the bound decreases by a factor of $\sqrt{10}$ for every 10~dB, as in~\eqref{eq:axial-peb}. With $\sigma_\theta=1^\circ$ it saturates: the median bound is 12.2~cm at 30~dB, 6.9~cm at 40~dB, 5.7~cm at 50~dB and still 5.4~cm at 70~dB. Beyond about 40~dB, a larger detector, a brighter LED or a quieter receiver no longer improves the accuracy, which is then set by the knowledge of the orientation.

Two rules follow for the design of a receiver. First, the tilt must be large enough for the orientation uncertainty: about $15^\circ$ for five orientations known to $1^\circ$, and less with more orientations or a better orientation sensor. Second, it must not exceed the support of the response minus the largest angle at which the LED is seen, $\theta\le a^{-1/2}-\phi_{\max}$ for the SQ response, so that every orientation keeps the LED in view. In the nominal scenario the two limits nearly meet: $\theta=15^\circ$ satisfies both, and the bound then stays below 9.6~cm at every position, with medians from 6.5~cm on the floor to 5.1~cm at 1.8~m. A working volume closer to the axis of the LED or a better orientation sensor widens this window. The receiver of the experiments is built with $\theta=15^\circ$ (Section~\ref{subsec:hardware}); the bound of the actual testbed, with its own geometry, calibrated model and measured noise, is examined in Section~\ref{subsec:res-bound}.
