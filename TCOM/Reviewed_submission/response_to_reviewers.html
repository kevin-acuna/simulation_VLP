Comment 3 — Table II: z-range and half-power angle selection

Table II: why do you use the z range of [0, 1.2] instead of a larger one such as [0, 2]? Which half-power semi-angle of the 3 values is selected in “optimization”?

Regarding the z-range: The transmitter is ceiling-mounted at z = 2 m, so z = 0 corresponds to the floor and z = 1.2 m corresponds to a receiver held at chest height. Extending the range to z = 2 m would place the receiver at the ceiling (transmitter height), which is physically unrealistic for a handheld or body-mounted device. Moreover, at very short transmitter–receiver separations (z close to 2 m), the incidence angle ψ approaches the FOV limit, the Lambertian model becomes ill-conditioned, and the FIM degenerates. The range [0, 1.2] m therefore covers the practical receiver-height envelope for indoor pedestrian scenarios.

Regarding the half-power angle: The GA optimization was run independently for each of the three values Φ1/2 ∈ {30°, 45°, 60°} listed in Table II, producing three sets of DEB-optimized orientations. Figure 4 (RMS-DEB/PEB vs. K) shows the results for all three. Based on this comparison, Φ1/2 = 45° was selected for the subsequent simulations because it offers a favorable trade-off between DEB performance and widespread availability in commercial LEDs.

The text accompanying Fig. 4 in Section IV now states: “Φ1/2 = 45° achieves competitive performance starting at K = 3 and, given its widespread use in commercial LEDs, is adopted for the subsequent simulations.”

Comment 6 — Fig. 4: why 60° half-power angle has larger PEB

Fig 4: I didn’t get why 60 deg half-power semi-angle has larger PEB. Doesn’t higher signal strength reduce PEB?

This is an insightful question. The intuition that “more power → lower PEB” holds for a fixed beam direction, but our system relies on angular discrimination between K orientations. The key is the Lambertian order m:

  • Φ1/2 = 45° → m ≈ 2.0 (more directive beam)
  • Φ1/2 = 60° → m ≈ 1.0 (wider, less directive beam)

The DEB and PEB depend on the gradient of the received power with respect to the direction, which appears in the FIM as the term m·Qim−1 (Eq. 17). A higher Lambertian order m produces steeper power variations as the beam is steered, amplifying the contrast between orientations and thus increasing the Fisher information. A lower m (wider beam) flattens the power profile, reducing the angular sensitivity: the K received powers become more similar regardless of the receiver direction, and the FIM shrinks.

In other words, while a wider beam distributes more total power across the room, it provides less directional information per orientation. Since the DEB/PEB depends on the sensitivity of power to direction (not on the absolute power level), a narrower beam (Φ1/2 = 45°) outperforms a wider one (Φ1/2 = 60°) for direction finding and positioning.

The text accompanying Fig. 4 in Section IV now explains: “Wider beams (Φ1/2 = 60°–75°) incur higher bounds due to the reduced Lambertian directivity”—we have ensured this explanation is clear.

Comment 7 — 2K unknowns vs. K measurements: underdetermined?

The orientation set optimization involves 2K unknowns (tilt-azimuth angles), while only K measurements are available. This results in an underdetermined problem, and the solution obtained by GA may not be reliable.

This comment conflates two fundamentally different problems:

  1. Offline orientation-set design (GA): This is a system-design problem, NOT an estimation problem. The 2K variables define the orientation set; for each candidate, the DEB is evaluated analytically over 1,792 positions using the closed-form FIM in Eq. (16). No measurements are involved—only theoretical bounds. The GA optimizes a deterministic scalar objective (Eq. 27) mapping 2K reals to a cost value—a standard global optimization problem.
  2. Online direction estimation: Once the set is fixed, the direction-finding stage estimates 2 DoF (direction on S²) from K power measurements. For K≥3, this is overdetermined (K measurements, 2 unknowns).

The analogy is antenna array design: the array geometry has many design parameters (element positions), fixed offline; the online estimation then has far fewer unknowns (angles of arrival).

The reliability of the GA solution is demonstrated empirically. Table III reports seven independently optimized orientation sets (K=3 to 9), all exhibiting physically interpretable patterns (e.g., K=4 yields four orientations at 29.9° tilt uniformly spaced in azimuth—the intuitively optimal symmetric configuration). Figure 2 compares each GA-optimized set against 50 random realizations, confirming that the optimized sets consistently reduce the median DEB by 49%–72% and yield tighter distributions across all K. These results provide strong evidence that the GA converges to high-quality solutions despite the non-convex landscape.

Comment 13 — NLS/Gauss-Newton gap with CRLB

Section VII-B-3) states that the NL estimator is implemented using Gauss-Newton iterations. Since Gauss-Newton is a typical implementation of MLE under Gaussian noise, it is expected to achieve the CRLB asymptotically. The observed performance gap requires further explanation.

The NLS is NOT the MLE and is therefore not expected to achieve the CRLB/PEB. The reasons are:

  1. NLS does not fit the full likelihood. It operates on max-normalized powers pi=μ̂i/maxjμ̂j (Eq. 29), which eliminates η (and hence distance information) by design. The MLE would jointly process all K+1 raw powers to estimate (nd, d) simultaneously; our NLS estimates only the direction from K normalized measurements.
  2. Two-stage decomposition loss. By decomposing direction finding and distance recovery into separate stages, the absolute power information (which carries distance information) is discarded during direction finding. The PEB assumes joint estimation from all K+1 measurements.
  3. PEB assumes perfect beam alignment. In the (K+1)-th measurement, the PEB assumes cosφ=cosψ=1 exactly, which no practical estimator can achieve since it relies on the estimated (imperfect) direction.
  4. Implementation: The NLS in the revised manuscript uses MATLAB's fmincon (SQP), not Gauss-Newton. We have corrected the text to remove any reference to Gauss-Newton to avoid confusion.

The performance gap is therefore structural (inherent to the two-stage architecture) rather than algorithmic. Importantly, when examining the direction-finding stage in isolation (which is the primary contribution), the gap narrows considerably: NLS achieves 0.63° RMSE versus the DEB of 0.52°—a factor of only 1.21× (Table II). This indicates that the NLS direction estimator operates close to the direction-finding CRLB. The larger gap observed in 3D positioning (NLS: 2.40 cm vs. PEB: 1.64 cm, factor 1.46×, Table IV) arises from the additional losses in the distance-recovery stage described above.

The text now explicitly states: "The performance gap arises from: (i) the two-stage architecture discards distance information during direction finding; and (ii) the PEB assumes perfect beam alignment in the distance-recovery measurement, which no practical estimator can achieve."

Section VII-B (text below Table IV) now explains the two sources of gap. Section V corrected to state the NLS uses constrained NLS (fmincon/SQP), not Gauss-Newton.

Comment 3 — Sensitivity to synchronization errors

We thank the reviewer for this practical concern. In the proposed architecture, the transmitter steers sequentially through K known orientations, and the receiver collects N power samples per orientation. Synchronization requires the receiver to know which orientation is active during each sampling window. We note the following:

  1. Timing structure: The transmitter follows a predetermined periodic scan pattern (K orientations per cycle). With state-of-the-art MEMS mirrors operating at kHz rates [Liu:25], each orientation dwell time is on the order of 1 ms. The receiver simply needs to associate each batch of N samples with the correct orientation index.
  2. Robustness of time-averaged measurements: The estimators operate on sample means μ̂i = (1/N)∑Pr,i,k (Eq. 5). Provided the majority of the N samples within each window correspond to the intended orientation, small timing offsets (i.e., a few samples acquired during transitions) are averaged out. This inherent averaging provides robustness to jitter.
  3. Standard synchronization mechanisms: Frame-level synchronization between transmitter and receiver is a well-studied problem in OWC systems [Haas:16]. Pilot symbols, header sequences, or a simple periodic beacon can achieve the required alignment without adding significant overhead.

A detailed Monte Carlo analysis of synchronization errors under various jitter distributions is an interesting direction. However, given the scope of the present contribution—which focuses on the theoretical framework, bound derivation, estimator design, and direction-finding analysis—and the availability of established OWC synchronization techniques, we believe that a full synchronization sensitivity study is best addressed in future experimental work, where real hardware timing impairments can be characterized directly.

Comment 4 — Multipath interference and reflection coefficients

The authors should supplement the current results with an analysis of positioning performance under different indoor reflection coefficients to evaluate the robustness of the model-based approach against multipath interference.

We appreciate this comment. The current model considers only the line-of-sight (LOS) channel, which is the standard approach in model-based OWP literature [Kahn1997, Zhuang2018, Wang2024b]. We justify this choice as follows:

  1. NIR wavelength: The system operates at near-infrared (NIR) wavelengths, where wall reflectivities are generally lower than in the visible spectrum, reducing NLOS contributions.
  2. Directional steering: The beam-steered LED produces a directional beam (Lambertian order m corresponding to Φ1/2=45°), which concentrates the transmitted power along the LOS path. The ratio of NLOS to LOS power is therefore small for most receiver positions within the room, particularly near the center.
  3. Ratio-based cancellation: The GLS/WLS estimators rely on power ratios between orientations. Since NLOS components (dominated by first-order wall reflections) change slowly with the transmitter orientation compared to the LOS component, the ratio-based formulation provides a degree of inherent robustness to multipath, as the NLOS contribution partially cancels in the ratio.

A systematic study of multipath sensitivity under varying reflection coefficients and room geometries would require integrating a frequency-domain or recursive NLOS channel model, which substantially increases the computational burden and is beyond the scope of the present contribution. We agree, however, that this is an important practical concern and have added it as a future work direction.

The Conclusion (Section VIII) now includes experimental validation as future work item (i), which will naturally address multipath effects in real environments. The LOS assumption is standard in the OWP literature and is explicitly stated in Section II-A.

Comment 9 — FIM rank conditions for near-coplanar orientations

The authors should analyze the Fisher Information Matrix rank conditions for cases where K ≥ 3 but the chosen orientations are nearly coplanar, as this affects the practical deployment rules.

This is an insightful observation. Section III-E (“Number of LED Orientations”) already discusses the three FIM regimes:

  • Underdetermined (K≤2): The FIM is rank-deficient and the CRLB diverges.
  • Just-determined (K=3): “With three distinct, non-coplanar LED orientations plus the range measurement, the total of four gradient vectors can span the 3D parameter space” (emphasis added). The text explicitly notes that the absence of redundancy makes the bound “highly sensitive to small perturbations in orientation geometry.”
  • Overdetermined (K≥4): The FIM accumulates redundant outer products, improving conditioning and reducing the PEB.

The near-coplanar case the reviewer raises corresponds to the degenerate K=3 scenario where the gradient vectors become nearly linearly dependent, causing the FIM to become ill-conditioned (large condition number). In the limit of exact coplanarity, the FIM loses rank and the CRLB diverges in the direction orthogonal to the common plane. This is precisely why Section III-E stresses the non-coplanarity requirement and why the GA optimization is essential: the GA inherently avoids degenerate configurations because they yield very large DEB values and are therefore penalized by the objective function (Eq. 27).

This is also visible in the optimized orientation sets (Table III): for K≥5, the GA consistently places orientations at two distinct tilt levels (one near 0° and one near 65°–70°), ensuring that the gradient vectors span three dimensions. The DEB heat maps (Fig. 3) further demonstrate that the optimized configurations maintain bounded DEB across the entire testbed, confirming that the GA avoids ill-conditioned geometries.
