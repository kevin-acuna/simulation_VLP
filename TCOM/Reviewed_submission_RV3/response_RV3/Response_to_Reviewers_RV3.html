Responses to the Editor

The revised manuscript has been improved, and the main technical concerns raised in the previous round have largely been addressed. In particular, the proposed two-stage procedure and the distinction between direction finding and distance recovery are now much clearer. One important consistency issue, however, remains. The direction-finding stage operates without receiver reorientation, whereas completion of the 3D position requires one cooperative PD reorientation for distance recovery. This distinction is correctly explained in several parts of the revised manuscript. However, statements such as “full 3D indoor localization without receiver rotation” still appear, particularly in the Conclusion. Such wording overstates the capability of the complete positioning procedure and may affect the credibility of the claimed practical advantage. This point should therefore be corrected consistently throughout the manuscript. The authors should also carefully address the remaining reviewer comments (see also the attached file) and clearly acknowledge the simulation-based nature and practical limitations of the present validation.

We would like to thank the Editor for the positive assessment of our revised manuscript and for this clear summary of the remaining points, which we address in detail in the following sections. We fully agree with the Editor’s remark on the remaining consistency issue. The main change made in this revision is to distinguish, with a single formulation used throughout the manuscript, the direction-finding stage—which operates with the receiver held at an arbitrary, unknown orientation—from the completion of the 3D position, which requires a single cooperative PD reorientation for distance recovery. The corresponding modifications, together with the explicit acknowledgment of the simulation-based nature and practical limitations of the validation, are summarized here:

  • Conclusion (Section VIII): “without receiver rotation” has been removed from the first paragraph, which now states the requirement of each stage (page 13); in the third paragraph, the simulations “support” the framework “within the scope of the simulation-based validation delimited in Section VII-D”, and experimental validation on a hardware prototype is listed as the first future work item (page 14).
  • Abstract (page 1): the cooperative measurement is described as one “in which the PD is reoriented once toward the LED”, and the tilt robustness is attributed to the direction-finding stage.
  • Section I: the paragraph preceding the contributions (page 2) and the first contribution (page 3) now mention the single cooperative PD reorientation of the distance-recovery step.
  • Table I (page 2): the entry of the proposed system is tagged with explicit “(D-F)” and “(3D)” labels, and its footnote has been rewritten.
  • Section VII-A-2 (page 10): the robustness to receiver tilt is stated to be specific to the direction-finding stage.
  • New Section VII-D, “Limitations of the Present Validation” (page 13): acknowledges the simulation-based nature of the results and the absence of experimental validation, states the system requirements presupposed by the validation (including the actuated receiver needed for the cooperative alignment), and delimits the receiver-orientation independence under NLOS multipath.
  • Responses to Reviewer 3: physical explanation of the WLS trend (Section VII-B), definition of the 14 dB operating point (Section IV), initialization of the genetic algorithm (Section IV), applications in edge computing and digital twins (Section I), and explicit tilt/azimuth labels in Table II.

Responses to Reviewer 3

We sincerely thank the reviewer for the careful reading of the revised manuscript and for the constructive comments, which have helped us clarify several technical points. Our responses follow.

1.   The reviewer recommends providing a physical or geometric explanation of why the WLS performance degrades when moving from K=5 to K=9 orientations in Table VI, as it seems counterintuitive compared to the GLS and NLS trends.

We thank the reviewer for this question. All \(K-1\) power ratios \(\hat\beta_i=(\hat\mu_i/\hat\mu_1)^{1/m}\) share the same reference measurement \(\hat\mu_1\). From (35), the error of each ratio contains an independent term \(n_i/\mu_i\) and a common term \(-n_1/\mu_1\), identical for all \(i\); combining (37)–(38), the ratio-error covariance has the exact diagonal-plus-rank-one form \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\beta\propto\operatorname{diag}(\beta_i^2/\mu_i^2)+\mu_1^{-2}\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathsf T}\), whose rank-one term is the noise of the shared reference. Geometrically, since \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_i=\mathbf{a}_i-\tilde n_i\mathbf{n}_{t,1}\) (Section VI-A), this common term displaces all constraint normals coherently along \(\mathbf{n}_{t,1}\) and rotates the estimated direction systematically; unlike independent errors, it does not average out when orientations are added. GLS accounts for this structure exactly through \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\beta^{-1}\), which attenuates the common component by \(1/(1+\sum_{i\ge 2}\mu_i^2/\mu_1^2)\), a factor that strengthens with \(K\). WLS retains only the diagonal and treats the single coherent perturbation as \(K-1\) independent ones; this mismatch grows with the number of ratios sharing the reference (4 at \(K=5\), 8 at \(K=9\)), which is also why Section VI-B recommends GLS when the cross-correlations are not negligible. We further note that the penalty is confined to the RMSE: in Table VI, the APE (2.25 → 2.15 cm) and the \(\mathrm{CDF}_{90\%}\) (4.49 → 4.01 cm) of WLS improve from \(K=5\) to \(K=9\), so the RMSE increase (2.92 → 3.42 cm) stems from a small subset of positions with large errors.

Section VII-B (page 12) now includes, after the sentence reporting the WLS degradation:

“These terms arise because all ratios share the measurement \(\hat\mu_1\) (38): its noise perturbs every constraint normal \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_i\) coherently along \(\mathbf{n}_{t,1}\) and hence does not average out with \(K\), so GLS suppresses it through \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\beta^{-1}\) whereas WLS weights it as independent noise—a mismatch that grows with the number of correlated ratios. The penalty is confined to the RMSE, as the APE and \(\mathrm{CDF}_{90\%}\) of WLS still improve from \(K=5\) to \(K=9\), i.e., it affects only a small subset of positions with large errors.”

2.   The reviewer suggests explicitly defining how the baseline SNR value of 14dB used in Section VII was chosen or whether it represents a typical operational indoor environment.

The value of 14 dB was not selected as a free parameter: it is the testbed-averaged SNR obtained from (6a)–(6c) with the nominal system parameters of Table II (\(P_t = 0.405\) W, \(A_{\mathrm{det}} = 4.8\times5.5\) mm2, \(R_p = 0.63\) A/W, \(\sigma_w^2 = 1.19\times10^{-14}\) A2), taken from the datasheets of the NIR LED and PIN photodiode considered, over the 1,792 receiver positions of the testbed. Since the operating SNR of a given deployment depends on the hardware and on the ambient-noise level, we do not claim that this value is universally typical; instead, the performance is characterized over SNR \(\in[10,50]\) dB for both the bounds (Fig. 5) and the estimators (Fig. 10), and Section VII-C explains how the SNR axis can be read in terms of ambient light.

Section IV (page 7), where the value first appears, now states:

“At a representative SNR of 14 dB, the \(K=5\) set achieves an RMS-DEB below 0.3° and an RMS-PEB below 1 cm. This value is the testbed-averaged SNR (6c) evaluated under the nominal system parameters of Table II.”

Section VII-C (page 12) now refers to this definition: “[…] at the nominal operating point (SNR = 14 dB; cf. Section IV) […]”.

3.   The reviewer thinks you should add a brief sentence in Section IV explaining how the initial population or search grid of the Genetic Algorithm was initialized to show it avoids getting stuck in local minima.

We thank the reviewer for this suggestion. The initial population of \(P=300\) individuals is generated by sampling each gene (tilt or azimuth) independently and uniformly at random within its interval of the bounded search range \(\Omega\) (Table II), which, for at most \(2K=18\) variables, provides a dense multi-point coverage of the search space. The population then evolves through stochastic uniform selection, scattered crossover (\(p_c=0.8\)), and adaptive feasible mutation, which preserves diversity throughout the run. Section IV (page 6) now reads:

“The initial population of \(P\) individuals is generated by sampling each gene independently and uniformly at random over its admissible interval in \(\Omega\), providing a dense, unbiased multi-point coverage of the \(2K\)-dimensional search space. Standard genetic operators—stochastic uniform selection, crossover with probability \(p_c\), and adaptive feasible mutation—are applied for at most \(G_{\max}\) generations, with early termination when the average relative improvement of the best fitness over \(G_{\max}\) generations falls below \(10^{-6}\). This population-based exploration, combined with the diversity preserved by the mutation operator, makes the search robust against entrapment in local minima.”

While revising this paragraph, the selection operator, previously described as “tournament selection”, was corrected to “stochastic uniform selection”, which is the operator used in the implementation.

4.   The reviewer recommends clarify whether the proposed direction-finding algorithms can still maintain their mathematical independence from the receiver orientation if the non-line-of-sight multipath reflections become dominant.

We thank the reviewer for this pertinent question. The independence established in Section VI-C relies on the LOS channel model (1)–(3), under which all receiver-dependent terms enter the \(K\) mean powers through the common factor \(\eta=C\cos\psi/d^2\), which cancels exactly in the power ratios (29) of GLS/WLS and in the normalization (24) of NLS. Under NLOS multipath, each received power also includes reflected contributions that depend on the LED steering orientation and on \(\mathbf{n}_r\); they do not share a common factor and therefore cancel in neither (29) nor (24). The independence then holds only approximately, and it is no longer guaranteed when NLOS reflections become dominant. In indoor optical channels with an unobstructed LOS, the LOS component dominates the reflected contributions [44]; the explicit modeling of NLOS propagation is left for future work. The manuscript has been modified in two places. Section VI-C (page 10) now closes with:

“Its validity under non-line-of-sight (NLOS) multipath is discussed in Section VII-D.”

and Section VII-D (page 13) contains the discussion:

“The LOS channel model also delimits the receiver-orientation independence of Section VI-C: under NLOS multipath, the received powers include reflected contributions that depend on both the LED steering orientation and \(\mathbf{n}_r\) and cancel in neither (29) nor (24), so the property holds only approximately and is not guaranteed when reflections dominate; in indoor channels with an unobstructed LOS, however, the LOS component dominates the reflected contributions [44].”
