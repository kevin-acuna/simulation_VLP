  1.       The transmission wavelength corresponds to infrared domain. Justify why and what LED or VCSEL technology is considered.

As indicated in response to the previous comment, we do not use VCSELs in this work, but rather LEDs, which do indeed emit infrared light. There are several reasons for this choice:

  • First, the test bench presented in the new experimental section (Section IV) was developed with an infrared LED rather than a VCSEL or laser source for photobiological safety reasons and because LEDs offer wider coverage due to their lower directivity.
  • Furthermore, we chose an infrared wavelength rather than a visible one because the optical source is coupled to a beamsteering system. A steerable visible source would generate a moving light flux visible to users, which is undesirable for obvious reasons of ambient lighting uniformity.

 

In order to clarify this hypothesis of an IR LED source and justify it, we have added a paragraph rephrasing the above explanations in Section II-B, just after equation (1).

In parallel, it should be noted that simulations similar to those presented in this article were also carried out with a laser source (modeled as having a Gaussian emission pattern), but we believe that this article is already quite long and that we cannot include them, given that under the same signal assumptions (signal bandwidth and noise level in particular), the trends observed with a Gaussian laser are similar to those reported in the article with a Lambertian LED. At the same time, the case of a laser or VCSEL source transmitting a signal of larger bandwidth (e.g. in the range of a few hundreds of MHz) would require changing the channel model to account for delay spreads, which is out of scope of this article (see also answer to Comment 3.c from Reviewer 2).

These justifications are provided at the end of Section II-B-1) in order to explain why we are only considering IR LEDs, while opening the discussion to these other types of optical sources.

  1.       The NLoS model corresponds to specular or diffuse reflections? In Fig. 1a seems that the authors are considering mirror or some reflecting material that may improve the received signal.

In Figure 1(a), the squares called ‘reflectors’ are not mirrors (i.e., they do not produce specular reflections) but elementary wall surfaces, which are therefore modeled as materials that induce diffuse reflections of the light to which they are exposed. In accordance with the literature on the subject, these elementary surfaces are modeled as passive Lambertian sources of order 1 with a specific reflectivity, generally 0.6, as explained in [32].

We then couple this modeling with the frequency-based approach proposed by Schulze in [27] in order to simulate indirect paths more quickly but nevertheless exhaustively compared with the time-based approaches commonly used in the literature (the reflections taken into account are thus not limited to order 1 or 2 but extend to infinite order).

However, it should be noted that the accuracy of indirect path modeling and simulation time depend heavily on the area of these elementary surfaces, so the value of this parameter must be chosen carefully, as specified in Section III-A.

To avoid any confusion about reflectors and to clarify the point raised by the reviewer, we have modified the text in Section II-B just above equation (5).

  1.       A scenario covering an area of 20m x 20 m is considered deploying a single AP. This assumption involves the lack of interference; however, this area typically requires multiple AP. Justify the deployment of a single AP.

The aim of this article is precisely to show that an OWC system with a single steerable optical source at the access point (AP) level is capable of covering a larger area than conventional systems based on fixed optical sources, each generating a fixed cell. The simulations and experiments carried out are intended to quantify the coverage gain achieved by beamsteering. This is why we chose a large area of 20 m x 20 m. These very large dimensions ensure that in most simulated cases (but not all, as suggested by the simulation results with OOK modulation), the coverage obtained will be less than 400 m², allowing us to observe the communication coverage provided by a single steerable AP. We have reworded the beginning of Section III-A to justify this assumption.

  1.       The noise n(t) is not defined. Is it thermal noise, shot noise, ambient noise?

In order to model noise in the simulations, we followed standard practices in the OWC-related literature, i.e., we considered that the noise at the OWC receiver consists of both thermal noise and shot noise.

In terms of shot noise, we neglected the contribution of the received data signal, which, according to the literature, is generally much lower than the shot noise caused by ambient light (please also refer to the answer to Comment 3.a from Reviewer 2 for further details about this assumption).

Under these conditions, thermal noise and shot noise can be modeled as additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) sources, the sum of which is also an AWGN source. We therefore included all these contributions in the variable and adopted a value for this variable taken from literature.

To clarify this point, we have added a dedicated paragraph in Section III-A.

  1.       The impact of misalignment can be minimized applying the concept of angle diversity receiver (ADR).

We fully agree with the reviewer. However, deploying such an approach through simulation and experimentation is not the subject of this article, which focuses instead on quantifying the gain in communication coverage obtained by using an optical beamsteering system at a single OWC AP. Nevertheless, we have mentioned this solution through references on the subject when commenting on the simulation results of the case with misalignment (see end of Section III-C and references [35] and [36]) to bring this topic to the attention of readers.

In general, the paper has potential to be published in IEEE ACCESS. However, it mixes several concepts that should be properly clarified and organized to generate an interesting contribution. I encourage to resubmit the manuscript once all these issues will be solved.

We would like to thank the reviewer for all of these constructive comments and hope that incorporating them into this new version of the article has improved its quality.

While the data rates of the different modulations considered are indeed different, this does not mean that comparing the performance of these different modulations is not fair. In this article, we have instead adopted a hardware-driven approach, considering that in practical systems, communications performance is primarily constrained by the available hardware. In our simulations, this translates into the usable bandwidth parameter, set to 30 MHz, as justified in response to the Comment 3.c from Reviewer 2 above. All other modulation design choices are then derived from this parameter, as detailed below.

Regarding OOK

We couple the OOK modulation with Manchester coding, which is quite common in OWC, in order to move most of the data signal spectrum away from DC levels and thus be able to use high-pass filtering (e.g., at 1 MHz) on reception to cut out ambient light without degrading the data signal. This results in a halving of the data rate relative to the bandwidth used, i.e., a rate of 15 Mbps (and not 10 Mbps as stated in the first version of the article, which was a typographical error for which we apologize).

We have added these details in the waveform generation process in Section II-C.1 and the corresponding data rate value in Section III-A and in Table I (which gathers former Tables I, II and III).

Regarding -CAP and OFDM

We set the remaining modulation parameters to allow for a fair comparison, i.e.:

  • We set the number of useful sub-bands or subcarriers to 15 in both cases. This results in 15 transmitted sub-bands with -CAP and 32 transmitted subcarriers with OFDM, due to the Hermitian symmetry and null subcarriers specific to DCO-OFDM.
  • We then distributed these sub-bands / subcarriers across the entire available spectrum so as to respect the orthogonality condition for DCO-OFDM (the subcarrier spacing is thus = 937.5 kHz) and so as to ensure a sub-band width of 1 MHz and a non-null guard band between sub-bands to avoid inter-band interference.
  • Each subcarrier / sub-band was then modulated with identical QAM modulations.
  • A cyclic prefix (CP) of length equal to ¼ of the OFDM symbol (i.e., 8 samples) was added to each DCO-OFDM symbol to mitigate inter-symbol interference (ISI). This function is dealt with by the pulse shaping filter in -CAP so that no CP is necessary with this scheme.

This results overall in an lower data rate with DCO-OFDM (from 45 Mbps to 112.5 Mbps) than with -CAP (from 60 Mbps to 150 Mbps), a difference mainly explained by the CP overhead.

  1.       “Misalignment” scenario and methodology: In Sec. IV‑A (p. 12), “misalignment” is modeled by pointing to four points on a circle of 20 cm radius around the UE (at z separation 1.65 m), i.e., ≈ 6.9° angular error. The coverage metric then takes the intersection across the four cases—effectively a worst‑case over discrete directions. This design is arbitrary and pessimistic. A more realistic analysis would: (i) model random angular errors with a variance that reflects tracking/IMU precision or beam quantization; (ii) report percentiles (e.g., 5/50/95%) rather than the hard intersection; and (iii) sweep misalignment statistics rather than a single 20 cm radius.

It is entirely true that the choice of the misalignment angle in the “misalignment” scenario is arbitrary and could have been done differently. For example, one of the following two approaches could have been used:

  • A first approach, probably the best one, consists in developing a statistical model of the impact of misalignment on the OWC channel gain when a Lambertian source is used, similar to what is done in [R1] for a Gaussian laser source. We have actually developed such a model, but presenting and using it in detail (i.e., including all the modeling, mathematical derivations and parametric study) in the article submitted here would have resulted in a manuscript that is too long and dense, even though the study of misalignment is not the core focus of this article. For these reasons, we did not use it here.
  • Another approach would have been to replicate the simulations based on the pointing radius (for example, by varying this pointing radius between a minimum and maximum value) rather than using a single fixed value. However, with the material resources at our disposal, each iteration takes approximately two days because calculating indirect paths is extremely time-consuming. A more comprehensive analysis of misalignment across the pointing radius would nevertheless involve simulating other pointing radius values, as well as other combinations of points on the same pointing circle (or even combinations of points from different circles), which would ultimately lead to very lengthy simulations. Again, given that the study of misalignment is not the focus of our article, we did not follow this method.

For these various reasons, we preferred to restrict simulations to four points on the same pointing circle with a pointing radius that we felt was both significant and not excessive, given the performance currently reported in the literature in terms of positioning and optical pointing.

The reviewer is also right to point out that defining communication coverage in “Misalignment” scenario as the intersection of the coverages according to the four pointing directions just mentioned is pessimistic. This assumption, although pessimistic, can nevertheless be justified by the fact that it is theoretically possible for the misalignment value to change during the reception of the same symbol. However, this change in misalignment can cause a drop in SNR and therefore BER such that the symbol will no longer be received correctly, thus implying a loss of communication even though the UE has not changed position. This assumption could certainly be nuanced by introducing a statistical dimension, which we have not done for the reasons mentioned above, but it is not without foundation. Furthermore, we believe it is intellectually honest to compare the ideal beamsteering case with a pessimistic misalignment case in order to bound the impact of misalignment on communication performance relative to the ideal case.

In order to better justify our definition of communication coverage in the “Misalignment” case, we have added and summarized the arguments above in Section III-C.

It is true that the maximum power values obtained may seem high, especially when compared with thresholds for class 1 lasers (according to IEC 60825-1), which are most likely better known to the OWC community than thresholds for LEDs. However, in the case of a Lambertian LED with large semi-angle at half power, the optical power is radiated in a wide solid angle, which reduces the radiance. In parallel, the corresponding optical signal is not coherent, which considerably reduces its danger compared with a laser source.
